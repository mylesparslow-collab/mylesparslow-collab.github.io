Field guide · Writing with AI · October 2026

Write the system before the story.

A how-to for writing novels and screenplays with AI: plan the book, build a story bible, draft one chapter at a time, check continuity against the bible, and revise like an editor. It is the workflow behind about 300,000 words of my fiction and screenwriting, set beside what the research says.

The AI drafts, but the choices stay with you. Every claim about research or policy links to its source. My own manuscripts are not quoted here; the screenplay documents are linked where they are already public.

01 · The short version

Ten rules for long-form writing with AI

If you read nothing else. Each rule is backed further down.

  1. Decide what it is about before it is written

    A one-line logline and a one-line theme. Without them the model writes something that sounds like a book and is about nothing.

  2. Outline in levels, and review each one

    Sentence, paragraph, page, scene list. Each review is where your taste enters. Research systems that plan in more detail stay more coherent.

  3. Keep one story bible and paste it every time

    Characters, world rules, timeline and a style sheet. It is the model’s only memory of your book between sessions.

  4. Use one AI to plan and another to draft

    A second model reads the plan cold, so gaps in it show up. In my workflow ChatGPT writes plans, outlines and research; Claude drafts from them.

  5. Draft one chapter at a time

    Ask for a single chapter or a 10 to 15 page sequence, with a hand-off note after it. Long runs drift.

  6. Material first, instruction last

    Put the bible and outline at the top and the request at the bottom. Anthropic measured up to 30% better answers on long inputs.

  7. Check continuity with quotes

    Ask the checker to quote the line and the bible entry it contradicts. A quote can be verified; a vague worry cannot.

  8. Diagnose before you rewrite

    Get editor’s notes first, decide what to fix, then revise. “Make it better” just gives you a different draft.

  9. Edit out the AI habits by hand

    Clichés, over-explaining, purple prose and vague detail. Professional writers preferred human edits to the AI’s own fixes.

  10. Know the rules before you publish

    Prompts alone don’t earn copyright in the US, and Amazon KDP requires you to declare AI-generated text.

02 · The pipeline

Six stages, each with its own document

Every stage ends in a document that the next stage reads. That is what makes a 190,000-word novel consistent across months of sessions: nothing important lives only in a chat history. The order is the one I use for novels and that my screenwriter prompt enforces for scripts.

1 · Premise

The idea interrogated until it has a protagonist, a want, a wound and a theme. Ends in a logline.

2 · Outline

Grown one level at a time to a beat sheet and a scene list, each level reviewed by you.

3 · Story bible

Characters, world rules, places, timeline, style sheet and voice samples. The source of truth.

4 · Draft

One chapter or sequence per request, from the bible and that chapter’s outline, ending in a hand-off note.

5 · Continuity

Each chapter checked against the bible and a running log. The log is updated before the next chapter.

6 · Revision

Notes first, then structural, scene and line passes, then your own edit in your own voice.

What the planning AI does

Asks questions, proposes options, expands outlines, writes research briefs and the bible. In my workflow this is ChatGPT.

What the drafting AI does

Writes prose from the documents, one chapter at a time, and reports what it set up or invented. In my workflow this is Claude.

What you do

Choose at every fork, approve each outline level, settle every continuity conflict, and make the final edit. The book is the sum of those choices.

Why it works

This is the same shape researchers converged on. Re3 plans, drafts, rewrites and edits in separate steps; DOC showed that a more detailed outline made stories more coherent (judged better on plot coherence 67.6% to 45.1%); DeepMind’s Dramatron builds a screenplay down from a logline to characters, beats, locations and dialogue. 03 · Plan and outline

Make the decisions while they are cheap

Changing a sentence in an outline costs a minute. Changing the same decision in chapter 20 means rewriting chapters 3 to 19. Do the thinking here.

Start by being interrogated, not by generating

The first prompt should make the AI ask you questions and attack the premise, not write story. Your answers become the raw material only you can supply. Models default to familiar tropes; professionals working with Dramatron called its output “provocations” rather than finished work, and noticed predictable tropes and bias.

Grow the outline one level at a time

Randy Ingermanson’s Snowflake Method is a good scaffold for this: one sentence, then a paragraph with three disasters and an ending, then a page, then character sheets, then a scene list. Ask the AI to expand exactly one level and mark what it invented with [NEW], so nothing slips in unreviewed.

Pin structure to length

A beat sheet with word or page targets shows a sagging middle before it is written. Blake Snyder’s fifteen Save the Cat beats are a common default for both novels and scripts. Use them as a checklist of turns, not a formula.

Research once, as its own document

If the book needs expertise (my novel’s prose uses real surveying technique), have the planning AI write a research brief for a fiction writer, with uncertain facts marked to verify. You check those yourself; the drafting AI only reads the checked version.

04 · The story bible

The model’s only memory of your book

A model starts every session knowing nothing about your book. The bible is what you paste so it knows. For my novel Thalweg (34 chapters and 6 interludes, 189,962 words), a 4,982-word bible came first and kept the book consistent across its length. Short enough to paste every time is the point.

SectionWhat goes inWhy the AI needs it
Pitch and themeOne paragraph and one sentenceEvery scene choice gets measured against what the book is about
CharactersRole, want, wound, how they talk with sample lines, what they know and whenStops voices merging and characters knowing things too early
World rulesWhat is possible, impossible, and what it costsInconsistent rules feel arbitrary, and readers notice
PlacesEach with a few concrete sensory detailsGives the model specifics in place of generic description
TimelineNumbered or dated events, backstory includedAges, travel times and seasons stay consistent
Style sheetPoint of view, tense, chapter length, banned words and habits, voice samplesThe voice is defined by you, not by the model’s default
Open questionsDecisions not yet madeThe model flags them instead of quietly inventing answers

Keep it living

After each chapter, update a separate continuity log and fold settled facts into the bible. If the two disagree, you choose which wins.

Where to keep it

A Claude Project or a ChatGPT Project holds it as a file every chat in the project can read. Writing tools build the same idea in: Sudowrite calls it a Story Bible, Novelcrafter a Codex.

Write it for the AI

Plain statements beat atmosphere. “Mara never swears; she says ‘fine’ when she is furious” is usable. A mood paragraph is not.

05 · Drafting

One chapter per request, with a hand-off note

The drafting prompt carries the bible, this chapter’s outline, and the end of the previous chapter. It asks for one chapter and stops. Then it asks for a short note outside the prose about what the chapter changed.

What goes in, in this order

  1. The story bible
  2. The scene list for this chapter only
  3. The last hand-off note and the previous chapter’s final paragraphs
  4. The request: which chapter, how long, where to stop
Why this order

Anthropic’s guidance for long inputs is to put the documents first and the question last, which it measured at up to 30% better answers. Research also finds models use information at the start and end of a long input better than the middle, so a short, focused paste beats the whole manuscript.

Size each request

A chapter of a few thousand words, or 10 to 15 screenplay pages, is a unit you can actually read and judge. My novel’s chapters ran from 4,176 to 8,701 words.

Ask for the hand-off note

Word count, what was set up, any new fact, and anything invented that the bible did not cover. My screenwriter prompt requires this after every sequence. It is the input to the continuity stage.

Lock the voice with samples

Paste two or three passages in the voice you want and have the model turn them into rules. Without samples you get the model’s house style, and more writers end up sounding alike.

Start fresh, not long

Long, corrected chats get worse. A new chat per chapter, fed from the documents, keeps quality steady and is why the documents matter.

06 · Continuity

A running log, checked chapter by chapter

Continuity errors are the most fixable problem in AI fiction, if you catch them per chapter. My screenwriter prompt keeps “a running continuity log of rules established, objects planted and characters alive”; the same log works for novels.

What the log tracks

  • Rules established, and when
  • Objects and clues planted, and whether they are paid off
  • Injuries, deaths, who is where
  • What each character knows
  • Dates and elapsed time

How to check

Paste the bible, the log and one chapter. Ask for each problem as a quote from the chapter, the conflicting entry, and a severity. Tell it to say “nothing found” rather than invent issues.

What to check across the book

Build a timeline table from chapter summaries, not the full text, and ask where someone acts on information they could not have had yet. Summaries fit; whole manuscripts get skimmed.

Don’t outsource the judgement

A checker model can miss things and can invent them. The quote requirement lets you verify each flag in seconds, and you decide every conflict between the log and the bible.

07 · Revision

Notes first, then passes from big to small

Revise the way an editor works: a diagnosis, then structure, then scenes, then lines. My novel’s chapters went through many rounds of this. The last pass is always yours.

1 · Notes, no rewriting

The big picture in three sentences, scene by scene turns, character logic, the worst lines quoted, and five prioritized fixes. This is the script doctor structure from my screenwriter prompt.

2 · Structural and scene passes

Fix what the notes found, in order. Re-run continuity after any change that moves a fact.

3 · Line pass, by hand

Use the AI to flag, not to rewrite. Accept edits one by one and reject any that sound more generic than what you had.

The seven habits to edit out

In the LAMP study, professional writers edited 1,057 paragraphs written by GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.1 70B, and agreed on seven kinds of problem. The pattern was similar across all three models, and experts still preferred human edits to the models’ own fixes.

HabitWhat it looks likeShare of edits
Awkward word choiceMisused words, unclear pronouns, needless passive voice28%

Unnecessary expositionExplaining what the scene already showed18%

08 · Screenplays: a worked case

A 16-section system prompt as the bible

For screenwriting, the system prompt plays the bible’s role for the craft, and the project brief plays it for the story. My horror screenwriter prompt is 4,642 words in 16 sections and turns Claude into a senior screenwriter and script doctor.

Read the system promptRead the CORPUS packageRead the FINE-TUNED pitch

What the prompt holds

Role and philosophy, a fear-mechanics toolkit, subgenre conventions, structure with page targets, character and dialogue rules, format, production awareness, content limits, a cliché list, a staged workflow with templates, a script doctor mode, a self-review checklist, and a project brief to fill in.

The staged workflow it enforces

Logline and theme, then threat bible, characters, beat sheet, step outline, pages in 10 to 15 page sequences with a continuity log, then revision against the checklist. It stops to confirm between stages.

Breadth, then a hard cut

CORPUS is a 5,351-word development package with a 15-beat sheet, 7 characters, and 9 set pieces chosen from 64 brainstormed. Generating many options and keeping few is where AI helps most; choosing is the human part.

Same story, different deliverable

FINE-TUNED is the 3,909-word pitch for the same idea, in 18 beats over three acts. Read side by side, the two show how the brief’s “deliverable” line changes what the same prompt produces.

Format tip

Ask for pages in Fountain, a plain-text screenplay format that screenwriting apps open directly, so drafts need no reformatting.

09 · Prompt library

17 prompts for every stage

They work in Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or a local model. Replace the {{placeholders}} and keep the parts that say where to stop. For general prompting advice, see the Prompting Field Guide.

Plan & outline · 01

Interrogate my premise

Before any outline, find out whether the idea can carry a book.

Here is a story idea I want to develop into a {{novel / screenplay}}:

<premise>
{{two or three sentences}}
</premise>

Act as a demanding development editor. Do not write any story yet.
1. Say in one sentence what this story is really about underneath the plot.
2. Name the protagonist's want and wound, or say that they are missing.
3. List the three weakest points of the premise, most serious first.
4. Ask me up to five questions whose answers would change the story the most.
Be blunt. If the idea is generic, say what would make it specific.

Answer the questions in your own words. Those answers are the first real material of the book.

Plan & outline · 02

Five loglines, then pick

Forces a one-sentence spine before the outline grows.

Using my premise and answers above, write five different loglines in this shape:

When {{protagonist with a specific wound}} {{inciting incident}}, they must {{goal}} before {{stakes}}, but {{complication that turns the wound into the weapon}}.

Make the five genuinely different: change the protagonist, the stakes or the genre blend, not just the wording. Under each, add one line on what kind of book it implies. Then tell me which one you would bet on and why.

The template is the one from my horror screenwriter prompt; it works for novels too.

Plan & outline · 03

Expand the outline one level at a time

Snowflake-style growth: sentence, paragraph, page, scene list.

Here is the approved logline and the one-paragraph summary:

<logline>{{logline}}</logline>
<summary>{{paragraph: setup, three disasters, ending}}</summary>

Expand each sentence of the summary into its own paragraph, so the whole story is about one page. Keep every turn I already decided. Mark anything you invented with [NEW] so I can accept or reject it. Do not go further than one page; we will expand again after I review.

Repeat at each level: one page, then four, then a scene list. Each review is where you steer.

Plan & outline · 04

Beat sheet with page or word targets

Pins the structure to a length, so the middle cannot sag unnoticed.

Turn the one-page outline into a beat sheet for a {{novel of about 90,000 words / feature screenplay of about 95 pages}}.

For each beat give:
BEAT NAME (target position: {{word count or page}})
What happens: one or two sentences.
What it does: the dramatic or thematic function.
What changes: how the situation is different at the end.

Flag any stretch longer than {{10% of the length}} where nothing turns.

Save the Cat's fifteen beats are a common default; use them as a checklist, not a cage.

Story bible · 05

Build the story bible

The single document every drafting session reads first.

From everything we have agreed (premise, logline, outline, character notes), write a story bible in Markdown with these sections:

1. One-paragraph pitch and the theme in one sentence
2. Characters: for each, role, want, wound, how they talk (with two sample lines), what they know and when
3. World rules: what is possible, what is not, and what it costs
4. Places: each with a few concrete sensory details
5. Timeline: dated or numbered events, including backstory
6. Style sheet: point of view, tense, chapter length, banned words and habits, three short passages showing the voice
7. Open questions I have not decided

Use only what we decided. Where something is missing, list it under Open questions instead of inventing it.

Keep it short enough to paste every time. A few thousand words can hold a long novel.

Story bible · 06

Research document for a technical world

When the book needs real expertise, gather it once and cite it.

My novel needs a believable grounding in {{field, for example surveying, 14th-century Naples, plastering}}.

Write a research brief for a fiction writer, not a textbook:
- the 15 to 25 facts, tools and terms a practitioner would actually use, each with a one-line plain explanation
- how the work feels day to day: sounds, smells, frustrations, slang
- common mistakes outsiders make when writing about it
- sources I should check myself for anything a plot point depends on
Mark anything you are not sure of as [VERIFY].

Check every [VERIFY] item yourself before it reaches the manuscript.

Drafting · 07

The drafting brief

One chapter or sequence at a time, with everything the model needs and nothing else.

<story_bible>
{{paste the bible}}
</story_bible>

<outline_for_this_chapter>
{{the scene list for chapter N only}}
</outline_for_this_chapter>

<previous_chapter>
{{hand-off note from chapter N-1, plus its last two or three paragraphs}}
</previous_chapter>

Write chapter {{N}}, about {{4,000 to 6,000}} words, following the outline exactly. Match the voice in the style sheet. End the chapter where the outline ends; do not start the next one.

After the chapter, outside the prose, add a hand-off note: word count, what this chapter set up, any new fact about a character, place or rule, and anything you had to invent that the bible did not cover.

Long material goes first and the instruction last, as Anthropic recommends for long inputs.

Drafting · 08

Voice lock from a sample

Hands the model your voice rather than its own.

Here are three passages written in the voice I want:

<voice_samples>
{{passage 1}}
{{passage 2}}
{{passage 3}}
</voice_samples>

Describe this voice in ten concrete rules (sentence length, rhythm, metaphor habits, what it never does, how dialogue is tagged). Then rewrite the paragraph below to follow the rules, changing as little of the content as possible:

<paragraph>{{draft paragraph}}</paragraph>

Save the ten rules into the style sheet in your bible.

Drafting · 09

Three ways into a scene

Use when a scene is flat; pick a direction before any prose.

The next scene in the outline is: {{scene summary}}.

Before writing it, give me three different ways to stage it. For each: where it is set, whose point of view, what the scene is really about underneath the dialogue, and the image it ends on. Make at least one of them the option a careful writer would not reach for first. Stop and wait for me to choose.

The CORPUS package came from this pattern at scale: 64 set pieces brainstormed, 9 kept.

Continuity · 10

Continuity check against the bible

Quote first, then judge, so the check is grounded in the text.

<story_bible>
{{bible}}
</story_bible>

<continuity_log>
{{running log: rules established, objects planted, injuries, who knows what, dates}}
</continuity_log>

<chapter>
{{chapter N}}
</chapter>

Check the chapter for continuity errors against the bible and the log. For each possible problem, first quote the exact sentence from the chapter, then quote the bible or log entry it conflicts with, then say how serious it is (breaks the plot / reader would notice / minor). Do not report style issues. If you find nothing, say so; do not invent problems.

Run it one chapter at a time. Facts buried in the middle of a very long paste get missed.

Continuity · 11

Update the bible and the log

Keeps the source of truth current after every chapter.

Here is the current continuity log and the hand-off note from chapter {{N}}:

<continuity_log>{{log}}</continuity_log>
<handoff>{{hand-off note}}</handoff>

Return the updated log only. Add new facts with the chapter number. Mark any fact that changed with CHANGED and the old value. List under "Decide" anything that contradicts the story bible, so I can choose which one wins.

You decide every conflict. The model only lists them.

Continuity · 12

Timeline and knowledge audit

Catches the errors readers love to find: who knew what, and when.

Across the chapter summaries below, build a table with one row per scene: chapter, in-story date or day, characters present, and what each character newly learns.

<summaries>{{one paragraph per chapter}}</summaries>

Then list every place where a character acts on information they could not have had yet, and every place where time does not add up (travel, ages, seasons, injuries healing).

Feed it summaries, not the whole manuscript, so the table fits and stays accurate.

Revision · 13

Notes before rewrites

Diagnose first. A rewrite without a diagnosis just produces a different draft.

Read this chapter as an editor would. Do not rewrite anything.

<chapter>{{chapter}}</chapter>

Give notes in this order:
1. The big picture in three sentences: what the chapter is trying to do, whether it gets there, and the single most important fix.
2. Scene by scene: does each scene turn? Where does tension drop?
3. Character: are choices believable and motivated?
4. Line level: the five worst sentences, quoted, with what is wrong with each.
5. The five changes that would improve the chapter most, in order.
Praise only what works and say why.

This is the script doctor structure from my screenwriter prompt, adapted for prose.

Revision · 14

AI-tell edit pass

Targets the seven habits professional writers flagged in AI prose.

Edit the passage below for these seven problems only: clichés; unnecessary or repeated exposition; purple prose; poor sentence structure; vague, unspecific description; awkward word choice; tense slips.

<passage>{{passage}}</passage>

Return a table: original phrase, problem type, suggested edit. Do not rewrite the whole passage, and do not touch sentences that have none of these problems. Keep my voice: if an edit makes it sound more like generic AI prose, leave the original.

The categories come from the LAMP study, where professional writers edited 1,057 AI paragraphs.

Revision · 15

Read-aloud dialogue pass

Finds lines nobody would say, and characters who sound alike.

Here is a scene. List each character's lines separately, then:
- flag any line a real person would not say aloud, and why
- flag any two characters who sound interchangeable, with an example
- flag dialogue that explains what the reader already knows
Suggest a fix only for the flagged lines, in each character's voice as described in the bible.

<scene>{{scene}}</scene>

Then read the flagged lines aloud yourself. Your ear is the real test.

Screenplay · 17

Step outline in Fountain-ready form

Every scene numbered, with the turn and the plants, before a single page.

From the approved beat sheet, write a step outline. For each scene:

#. INT./EXT. LOCATION - DAY/NIGHT
Who: characters present.
What happens: two or three sentences.
The turn: how the situation is different at the end.
Plants or payoffs: rules, objects or lines set up or paid off.

Flag any scene with no turn, and any payoff with no plant.

When you draft pages, ask for Fountain format so the text drops straight into screenwriting apps.

10 · Limits, rights and disclosure

What AI cannot do for you, and what you owe readers

This is a summary of public research and policy, not legal advice. Check the current rules for your publisher and country.

Raw AI fiction still falls short

In a CHI 2024 study, ten creative writers judged 48 stories against 14 tests of creative writing. Stories written by LLMs passed 3 to 10 times fewer tests than stories by professionals, and no model’s ratings matched the experts’. The workflow exists to close that gap with your judgement.

AI ideas make stories more alike

In a study of 300 writers, AI ideas raised the novelty of individual stories (most for less creative writers), but stories that used an AI idea were 10.7% more similar to each other. Protect what is specific to you: your premise, your research, your voice samples.

Copyright needs human authorship

The US Copyright Office’s January 2025 report says prompts alone are not enough for protection. Human-authored expression that is perceptible in the work, and creative selection, arrangement or modification of AI output, can be protected, and using AI to assist does not bar copyright.

Amazon KDP wants a declaration

KDP requires you to declare AI-generated text, images and translations, even if you edited them heavily. AI-assisted work, where you wrote it and used AI to edit, refine or check it, does not need declaring.

Authors Guild best practice

Keep the ideas and wording yours if you claim authorship, disclose substantial AI use to readers and your publisher, check your contract’s originality warranty, and don’t use AI to imitate another living writer’s style.

Screenwriters under the WGA

Under the 2023 WGA agreement, AI is not a writer and its output is not literary or source material. A company can’t require a writer to use AI, must disclose AI-generated material it hands over, and a writer may use AI with the company’s consent.
