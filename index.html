The problem

Before a firm takes a matter, it must check every party against everyone it has represented or opposed. Mid-size firms usually search their billing system letter for letter, so they miss parent companies, former names, nicknames and misspellings. Intake is a web form nobody connects to the search, and the enterprise tools are quote-only and installed by consultants.

The people involved have different needs. An associate submits the request, a conflicts analyst reviews the hits, practice leaders and general counsel approve, and finance opens the matter. Conflara gives each of seven roles its own view.

How the matching works

  • Names are normalized: corporate suffixes stripped, abbreviations expanded, initials collapsed so "A.B.C. Holdings" finds "ABC Holdings".
  • Three scorers combine: a phonetic key (Smith and Smyth), Jaro-Winkler for typos, and trigram overlap for spacing. Rare words weigh more than common ones.
  • People match on surname first, then first name with 30+ nickname pairs (Kate and Katherine), aliases and former names.
  • Strong matches expand two hops through corporate families and one hop through personal links such as spouses and officers.

Decisions that come from knowing the work

Risk follows the relationship

A name match alone means little. Being adverse to a current client on an open matter is high risk; a closed matter is medium; a match only through a personal link is capped below high. Related parties with no matter history are dropped as noise.

Rules are built in

Former clients point to Rule 1.9, prospective clients to Rule 1.18, lawyer relationships to California Rule 1.7(b) and (c) disclosures. Lateral hires get the 1.10, 1.11 and 1.12 analysis, and engagement letters check the California fee-agreement statutes.

Lawyers decide, software suggests

The local AI pre-sorts hits and drafts memos, but every AI-filled field carries a mark until a person confirms it. Restricted and walled data never reaches the model, and accepting a suggestion never decides a hit.

Least privilege by role

Approvers see a summary and a sign-off. Requesters see no firm records while they type. Walls are enforced in what the server sends each user, and every blocked attempt is logged.

An audit trail that proves itself

The log is append-only with a hash chain and a "Chain intact" check, and database triggers refuse edits. Matters cannot close until the final bill is issued and trust is at zero.

Speed for the analyst

J and K move between hits, 1 to 5 record a decision, Enter accepts the suggestion, and earlier decisions on the same party appear inline.

Since then: scale, outside data and a private model

Conflara now has about 110,000 lines of TypeScript, 932 tests across 66 files and 140 commits, with roughly 40 of those commits and 13 merged pull requests landing in a single day. It runs two ways from the same code: a browser-only demo on fictional data, or a firm server with its own database, single sign-on, email and an AI model on the firm's own hardware.

Built for a firm with millions of names

Row-level storage, incremental sync and server-side search replaced loading everything into the browser. A repeatable benchmark on synthetic firms (invented names only) goes up to 3,000,000 parties, with a written account of what limited a large firm and what changed.

Outside entity data, watched nightly

A party can be linked to its public-register record: legal-entity data and SEC filings, including subsidiaries read from the latest annual report. A person chooses which names and parents or subsidiaries to add. A nightly job lists what changed for a person to accept or set aside, and cleared requests are flagged for another look. The docs say plainly which sources are built, tested against recorded answers, or only stubbed.

Search that is honest about itself

Every search gets a number and shows what it covered and where its limits are. An empty or missing source is never shown as clearance, hits carry plain labels such as "likely same entity" instead of scores, and a no-match result is neutral, not green.

Faster hit review

A compact hit list with sticky party headers, identity review kept apart from the conflict assessment, and follow-ups tracked until resolved. The suggested order is learned locally from the firm's own past decisions, shows its factors and counts, is off by default, and never hides a hit or records a decision. An offline evaluation of the ranking is written up in the repo.

Confidentiality and lateral hires

Confidential matters are searched in batch without being revealed: the user is told there is a confidential match and nothing more. Lateral hires get a pre-start checklist with distinct pending, blocked, not-applicable and complete states, each backed by evidence.

Compliance records

Required documents per request, a summary of each conflict, and tracking for business-associate agreements, joint-representation consents and founder acknowledgements.

Measured, not claimed

  • Name search (median): 77 ms to 1.3 ms at 50,000 parties; 535 ms to 5.3 ms at 250,000.
  • Submitting an intake: 1.5 s to 37 ms at 50,000; 8.0 s to 20 ms at 250,000.
  • After a colleague's change, a browser downloads 1 KB instead of reloading 200 KB.
  • Server memory at 250,000 parties: 5.4 GB down to 1.8 GB.

All figures come from a benchmark script run on synthetic firms with invented names.

A model that stays on the firm's hardware

The AI runs through Ollama or any OpenAI-compatible local server, so client names never leave the building. It triages hits with a short reason, finds parties in a matter description or pasted email (any name that is not in the text is discarded), drafts a conflicts memo from the recorded decisions only, and suggests column mapping for imports. Every use is written to the audit log with the model name. The ranking model is a separate local statistical fit that uses no AI provider.

Teaching and testing inside the app

  • Role-based walkthroughs (open a matter, prepare a request, approve, clear, get your firm live) that point at things but never click, submit or decide.
  • A first-week checklist that ticks itself off from what the person actually does, with tests that limited roles are never shown a step for a page they can't open.
  • A test environment on a fictional firm, marked by a striped banner on every page. It never writes to live data, sends email or calls an outside AI provider.

Support without sharing firm data

An administrator builds a support bundle of counts, settings switches, versions and error messages with names replaced by placeholders, reads it, then saves it. Nothing is sent automatically, and saving is written to the audit trail. To see a problem happen, support uses a test firm of made-up records at the same size and shape. The live app opens empty, with no sample data, and the desktop app ships for Windows, macOS and Linux with a first-run setup.

Taking it to market

  • Research. 14+ competitors compared from public sources, 21 gaps ranked, and an opening found: nothing credible is priced between about $250 a year and an enterprise quote.
  • Pricing. Published per-lawyer tiers at $25 and $45 a month, with staff users free. A 30-lawyer firm on the higher tier comes to $16,200 a year.
  • Pilot. Five firms over 90 days in four phases, measured on intake-to-cleared time, analyst hours per check and hits the old system missed.
  • Honesty. A security table that says what is built and tested, built but untested, documented only, and not started.

It began as the Conflicts Desk prototype in Claude Code, with tests and CI, and grew into Conflara. I supplied the domain: how conflicts departments actually run, which rules apply, and where existing tools fall short. Claude built the search engine, the synthetic firm and every screen. It then helped research competitors, price the product, write the pilot pack and script and code both films.

The problem

Jobs, phased payments and expenses lived in a spreadsheet. Receipts, client payments and new leads arrived by email. Estimates, contracts and change orders were written by hand, and nobody could say what a job had cost in labor until it was over.

The constraints shaped everything. The owner is not a programmer, client data had to stay on his computer, and nothing could reach the books or a client without his say.

What it does

Inbox to books

Every 30 minutes a local model reads the inbox read-only and returns a fixed JSON shape at temperature 0. Plain code then matches each receipt, payment or lead to a job: a job ID in the email first, then a unique street address, then a unique client surname. If two jobs fit, it asks.

Labor cost per job

Labor per crew-hour is computed from the estimate's own crew and duration lines, with part-timers counted as half. It warns below the floor rate, compares spend with the estimate's budget, and switches to logged hours once they pass the guess.

Money that adds up

Job IDs, payment IDs and change-order IDs follow one scheme and double as invoice numbers. Payment schedules are generated by rule and tested to the cent. A change order signed with a finger on a phone updates the contract and schedule together.

Hands-free input

Any big text box takes speech through a local Whisper model of about 150 MB. Receipt photos and insurance certificates are read by a local vision model, with the job picked by the same rules as email.

Runs itself

A double-click installer needs no admin password, sizes the models to the Mac's memory, and sets up five background jobs: the app, the mail check, a Monday report, a 6 AM crew sheet and the model server. Daily backups keep 30 copies, and a doctor command checks everything.

Built from research

A market study of the local remodeling trade set the pricing checks: industry margins imply a 43% materials markup rather than 20%, and the crew-hour floor comes from local wage and billing data.

Decisions behind it

  • The model drafts, code decides. Prices, totals, dates, IDs and job matching are computed in code. If a model's draft drops a required fact, the app falls back to a plain template.
  • Fewer fields. A sentence like "Home Depot 109.91 studs for Smith" fills in an expense. Every page keeps a by-hand form for when the model is off.
  • Nothing sent for him. Emails open in his mail app to read and send. Reminders never mention fees or lawyers.
  • Readable by design. The redesign cut 14 tabs to 5 groups, set text at 18px with nothing under 15px, and made every control at least 48px tall.

Where it goes next

A productizing plan finds about 70% of the app is generic, with the owner-specific parts being configuration rather than architecture. The plan is to treat this business as customer number one, then offer done-for-you setups to three to five local remodelers, with a pilot kit and sales sheet ready.

How Claude was used

Built in Claude Code from the owner's real estimates, contract terms and tracker. Claude wrote the code and tests within rules I set. The repo also carries a guide written for the next Claude session on the owner's Mac. It sets out how to explain changes in plain words, back up before any change, keep database migrations additive, test on a copy, and never edit contract text without review.

The atlas

Drawn as a mosaic

Territory is one fixed grid of tesserae, each slightly varied in shade, with grout between them. The empire is imperial purple edged in gold; the sea is blue tile. A Borders mode switches to clean outlines, and light and dark themes each have their own palette.

Conquest you can watch

When borders change, only the tiles that changed owner turn over, in a wave that starts from the conqueror's side, so territory is taken rather than repainted. Cities that change hands send out a ripple.

Built for scrubbing

Drag, click or use the keyboard; play at 1 to 50 years a second; or press Next change to jump to the next year the map moves. Ownership is cached per snapshot and neighbours precomputed, so fast scrubbing stays smooth.

Honest about the record

Overlapping claims of more than about 500 km² become hatched contested land instead of being handed to one side. Five places where the source disagrees with history, from Manzikert to 1453, are corrected and explained on the map.

Checked, not assumed

An automated check confirms zero invalid geometries and zero overlaps across all 200 snapshots, and ten key years match the source to within 1% of land area. Five other map sources were evaluated and rejected, each with a written reason.

More ways in

Compare two years side by side with a draggable divider, switch to a table of powers, toggle cities, battles and campaign routes, and share any view by its URL. Patterns stand in for colour, and reduced motion swaps flips for fades.

The encyclopedia around it

  • 464 entries pinned to fixed Wikipedia revisions with restored footnotes, plus 168 original introductions labelled as such.
  • 1,174 Commons images with author and licence; 41 were left out because their licence couldn't be confirmed.
  • A guide to all 440 episodes of The History of Byzantium podcast. The build fails if any 12-word run matches the podcast's pages.
  • 87 battle entries, a people index of 1,765, a gazetteer, coins, a primary-source reader for Procopius and Anna Komnene, and a timeline that shares its playhead with the atlas.

Built and verified

React, TypeScript and d3-geo, with Python pipelines for the map geometry: borders from the Seshat Cliopatria dataset, coastlines from Natural Earth, and 96 cities and 71 battles from Wikidata. It ships with 20 slider tests, 60 screenshot checks across phone and desktop, and Lighthouse mobile scores of 93 to 96.

How Claude was used

One long Claude Code session took it from an empty folder to a verified site. Claude evaluated map sources and their licences, built three data pipelines and the renderer, and wrote its own reports on what failed, what was skipped and where the sources disagree, for me to review.

The song workflow

The songs are written with Suno, and the process is a written system: concept, style, structure, meter, hook, verses, then a rhyme audit and a length audit before anything is generated. After generating, the song is diagnosed before it is rewritten. The full workflow runs to 17 steps; these are the ideas that matter most.

Start with the premise

Name the core concept, point of view, emotional tone and the central joke or tension, and brainstorm scenes and images before any lyric. Comedy works best when the singer treats an absurd premise completely seriously. The joke is never explained; it comes out of the character's logic.

A separate style prompt

Genre, BPM, drum feel, bass, harmony, instruments, vocal register and timbre, texture, space and mood, in under about 1,000 characters. The instrumental should sound like a real record first: future garage, neo-soul, dub, dancehall, vintage calypso, synth-funk, jazz-funk. Funny lyrics don't need funny production, and the contrast is often what makes them funny.

Lock structure, then meter

Sections are decided before writing, and equivalent sections get equivalent line counts (8, 12 or 16, never a verse that runs long because it has more jokes). A syllable pattern is set per section, such as 8/8/8/8 or 7/10/7/10, with an even stricter meter in the chorus because the music model needs a predictable shape.

The hook is designed on its own

A short repeated phrase of three to five syllables with a repeated grammatical construction and simple words: "Kitty computer", "No teef, big life". The chorus gets its own identity instead of summarising the verses.

Verses are distinct scenes

Concrete images, physical detail and character-specific logic. When several characters sing, each has a different worldview, vocabulary and kind of joke, and no two are interchangeable.

Rhyme with rules

End, internal, multisyllabic and slant rhyme, with several rhyme points inside a line when it comes naturally. No word rhymed with itself, no lazy pseudo-rhymes such as side and outside, and no bent syntax to force a rhyme. Sound beats spelling.

Written to be sung

Where do the stresses fall, can the singer breathe, are the vowels good for held notes, and does each line have a rhythmic shape like its neighbour? A lyric can look clever on paper and still generate badly if the meter is unstable.

Realistic runtime, musical endings

The target is about 3:15 to 3:30. If a lyric is too long, cut the weaker jokes and shorten the bridge rather than rush the model. Endings are a refrain, a stripped hook, a harmony stack or an instrumental fade, not a spoken skit.

Arrangements that evolve

Dropouts, countermelodies, filtered passages, bass breaks, drum changes, vocal stacks, dub-delay throws, sparse bridges and a bigger final chorus, so the track develops instead of looping. Vocals are described precisely, for example a high, clear, intimate lead with airy falsetto edges, close-mic phrasing, whispered doubles and soft stacked harmonies.

Quality control before output

Fifteen checks run on every lyric: equal line counts in matching verses, the intended syllable pattern, a consistent chorus meter, a short memorable hook, no self-rhymes or lazy rhymes, internal and multisyllabic rhyme present, no line much denser than its neighbours, a lyric that scans out loud, distinct verses, a realistic runtime, a joke that is embodied rather than explained, a musical ending, and a production prompt that sounds like a real record. The output is always two copyable blocks, a style prompt and the lyrics, with no long explanations.

Diagnose before rewriting

  • Confused melody: check syllable count and line length.
  • Weak chorus: simplify it and repeat more.
  • Rushed verses: cut density or cut lines.
  • Feels childish: make the production more serious.
  • Forced jokes: go back to character perspective and concrete detail.
  • Weak rhymes: rewrite the rhyme structure instead of patching words.

Cat Music Video Studio

A toolkit that connects a recorded song to a timed storyboard and animated character performances. Turning a finished song into a video means coordinating several timelines at once: the recording, the lyrics, the characters' performances and the visual story. Written lyrics don't capture how a singer actually delivers them, and repeated hooks, held vowels, pauses, ad-libs and changed pronunciations all make synchronisation hard. I directed the AI-assisted build of the tool that solves it.

An interactive review tool

One browser workspace holds the song, the isolated vocal, the lyrics and the storyboard. Words and lines are clickable to audition them, master and vocal switch without losing your place, and playback speed slows difficult phrases. A waveform seeks on click, the current word highlights live, and search and section filters move through verses and choruses. Review flags and confidence labels mark uncertain alignment, and a storyboard view ties timed scenes to actions, camera direction, continuity notes and generation prompts.

Audio analysis and alignment

Scripts decode the master and the isolated vocal, measure both, and compare sync at several points. Whisper recognition and reference-conditioned alignment propose word and line timings, and separate analysis of each stem gives evidence to compare them. Targeted passes handle the hard parts: repeated calls, short interjections and closing phrases. The data keeps supplied lyrics, model recognition, candidate timestamps and unresolved events apart, so uncertainty stays visible.

Production data other tools can use

JSON for timing maps, audio metadata, lyrics, scenes and review evidence. CSV for word and line timings, sections, non-lyric intervals, scene plans and review logs. SRT for draft captions. HTML for an interactive review package with the timing data embedded. Together they give audio, shots, captions and animation one shared timeline.

A finished animation

A complete music video built in the browser with p5.js, combining character drawings, environments, timed shots, captions and the original song. Mouth opening follows word timing and vocal amplitude, the mouth closes between timed words, and singing can be switched on or off per shot. Mouth and tongue shapes respond to the resulting value. It has play, seek, restart, mute, caption and fullscreen controls, plus a rendered vertical-video version.

  • "Every Lane, Every Road": a 34-shot animated video, about 3 minutes 33 seconds, exported at 1080 × 1920 and 25 fps.
  • "Why You Run Me Down, Newty?": 627 word records and 44 proposed shots.
  • "Mommy Save Mi": 567 word records and 42 proposed shots.

What it can't do yet

Alignment is provisional until someone has reviewed it by listening. The mouth animation is stylised and driven by words and vocal energy, so it is not phoneme-verified lip sync. The review interface supports inspection, but editable timing boundaries and saved corrections are still to come, and the processing scripts hold song-specific settings that need adapting for each new song.

Next: a reusable pipeline with configurable song inputs, persistent timing corrections and per-character mouth controls.

Built with

Python, Whisper small.en, stable-ts, FFmpeg, NumPy, SciPy, soundfile, JavaScript, HTML and CSS, and p5.js.

The model drafts, code decides
