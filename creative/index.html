Suno + animation

A repeatable way to write songs with Suno, and a toolkit that turns a finished song into a timed storyboard and an animated music video starring my cats.

Watch all 14 songs on YouTubeStart with one video

The song workflow

The songs are written with Suno, and the process is a written system: concept, style, structure, meter, hook, verses, then a rhyme audit and a length audit before anything is generated. After generating, the song is diagnosed before it is rewritten. The full workflow runs to 17 steps; these are the ideas that matter most.

Start with the premise

Name the core concept, point of view, emotional tone and the central joke or tension, and brainstorm scenes and images before any lyric. Comedy works best when the singer treats an absurd premise completely seriously. The joke is never explained; it comes out of the character's logic.

A separate style prompt

Genre, BPM, drum feel, bass, harmony, instruments, vocal register and timbre, texture, space and mood, in under about 1,000 characters. The instrumental should sound like a real record first: future garage, neo-soul, dub, dancehall, vintage calypso, synth-funk, jazz-funk. Funny lyrics don't need funny production, and the contrast is often what makes them funny.

Lock structure, then meter

Sections are decided before writing, and equivalent sections get equivalent line counts (8, 12 or 16, never a verse that runs long because it has more jokes). A syllable pattern is set per section, such as 8/8/8/8 or 7/10/7/10, with an even stricter meter in the chorus because the music model needs a predictable shape.

The hook is designed on its own

A short repeated phrase of three to five syllables with a repeated grammatical construction and simple words: "Kitty computer", "No teef, big life". The chorus gets its own identity instead of summarising the verses.

Verses are distinct scenes

Concrete images, physical detail and character-specific logic. When several characters sing, each has a different worldview, vocabulary and kind of joke, and no two are interchangeable.

Rhyme with rules

End, internal, multisyllabic and slant rhyme, with several rhyme points inside a line when it comes naturally. No word rhymed with itself, no lazy pseudo-rhymes such as side and outside, and no bent syntax to force a rhyme. Sound beats spelling.

Written to be sung

Where do the stresses fall, can the singer breathe, are the vowels good for held notes, and does each line have a rhythmic shape like its neighbour? A lyric can look clever on paper and still generate badly if the meter is unstable.

Realistic runtime, musical endings

The target is about 3:15 to 3:30. If a lyric is too long, cut the weaker jokes and shorten the bridge rather than rush the model. Endings are a refrain, a stripped hook, a harmony stack or an instrumental fade, not a spoken skit.

Arrangements that evolve

Dropouts, countermelodies, filtered passages, bass breaks, drum changes, vocal stacks, dub-delay throws, sparse bridges and a bigger final chorus, so the track develops instead of looping. Vocals are described precisely, for example a high, clear, intimate lead with airy falsetto edges, close-mic phrasing, whispered doubles and soft stacked harmonies.

Quality control before output

Fifteen checks run on every lyric: equal line counts in matching verses, the intended syllable pattern, a consistent chorus meter, a short memorable hook, no self-rhymes or lazy rhymes, internal and multisyllabic rhyme present, no line much denser than its neighbours, a lyric that scans out loud, distinct verses, a realistic runtime, a joke that is embodied rather than explained, a musical ending, and a production prompt that sounds like a real record. The output is always two copyable blocks, a style prompt and the lyrics, with no long explanations.

Diagnose before rewriting

  • Confused melody: check syllable count and line length.
  • Weak chorus: simplify it and repeat more.
  • Rushed verses: cut density or cut lines.
  • Feels childish: make the production more serious.
  • Forced jokes: go back to character perspective and concrete detail.
  • Weak rhymes: rewrite the rhyme structure instead of patching words.

Concept, style, structure, meter, hook, verses, rhyme audit, length audit, generate, diagnose, revise.

Cat Music Video Studio

A toolkit that connects a recorded song to a timed storyboard and animated character performances. Turning a finished song into a video means coordinating several timelines at once: the recording, the lyrics, the characters' performances and the visual story. Written lyrics don't capture how a singer actually delivers them, and repeated hooks, held vowels, pauses, ad-libs and changed pronunciations all make synchronisation hard. I directed the AI-assisted build of the tool that solves it.

An interactive review tool

One browser workspace holds the song, the isolated vocal, the lyrics and the storyboard. Words and lines are clickable to audition them, master and vocal switch without losing your place, and playback speed slows difficult phrases. A waveform seeks on click, the current word highlights live, and search and section filters move through verses and choruses. Review flags and confidence labels mark uncertain alignment, and a storyboard view ties timed scenes to actions, camera direction, continuity notes and generation prompts.

Audio analysis and alignment

Scripts decode the master and the isolated vocal, measure both, and compare sync at several points. Whisper recognition and reference-conditioned alignment propose word and line timings, and separate analysis of each stem gives evidence to compare them. Targeted passes handle the hard parts: repeated calls, short interjections and closing phrases. The data keeps supplied lyrics, model recognition, candidate timestamps and unresolved events apart, so uncertainty stays visible.

Production data other tools can use

JSON for timing maps, audio metadata, lyrics, scenes and review evidence. CSV for word and line timings, sections, non-lyric intervals, scene plans and review logs. SRT for draft captions. HTML for an interactive review package with the timing data embedded. Together they give audio, shots, captions and animation one shared timeline.

A finished animation

A complete music video built in the browser with p5.js, combining character drawings, environments, timed shots, captions and the original song. Mouth opening follows word timing and vocal amplitude, the mouth closes between timed words, and singing can be switched on or off per shot. Mouth and tongue shapes respond to the resulting value. It has play, seek, restart, mute, caption and fullscreen controls, plus a rendered vertical-video version.

  • "Every Lane, Every Road": a 34-shot animated video, about 3 minutes 33 seconds, exported at 1080 × 1920 and 25 fps.
  • "Why You Run Me Down, Newty?": 627 word records and 44 proposed shots.
  • "Mommy Save Mi": 567 word records and 42 proposed shots.

Built with

Python, Whisper small.en, stable-ts, FFmpeg, NumPy, SciPy, soundfile, JavaScript, HTML and CSS, and p5.js.

It was built through an iterative ChatGPT and Codex workflow, which wrote the implementation, analysis scripts, production documents and animation code.

What the local models do

Model manager

Pull, load, unload and inspect models from Ollama or any OpenAI-compatible local server. Idle models unload themselves, and a Mac Health page logs memory every minute so you can see what filled it.

Chat and compare

Stream any model with full controls and tokens per second, drop in PDFs or Word files, or send one prompt to several models side by side. A scoreboard tracks speed and thumbs up or down for each model.

Shared memory

One store of facts that every AI app on the Mac can read, reached through a small MCP server written for it, with per-app permissions. A local embedding model flags duplicates and conflicts using measured thresholds.

Ask your chats

Keyword search over every saved conversation, then a local model answers from the best passages.

Voice, images and songs

Replies read aloud with local voice cloning, speech input through whisper.cpp, pictures through local Stable Diffusion, and a Song Studio where a local model writes lyrics that another model sings.

The Agent Factory

A pixel-art factory floor where you hire cat agents, give each a model, prompt and tools, and chain them on a conveyor belt, such as research, then write, then edit.

The games

Nine games, all original code and pixel art: Kitty Trivia, Cat Care (feed and dress the cats; 24 accessories so far), Fish Tank (each species breeds the way it really does), BalCatro (a poker roguelike), Nine Lives (a dungeon crawler), Purrimeter (tower defence), Duck Pounce, Krillion (a daily word game) and CatGPT Radio. There's also Cat Beats, a beat maker with a step sequencer, piano roll and mixer, where a local model can produce a beat for you.

Series · Historical fiction

No Ear Cut Twice

94,828 words

On an island where perjurers lose their ears, a plasterer hides that his are prosthetic stucco, and a single lie blooms into a herd of thousands.

Corrado Vessa, a Neapolitan plasterer, swore a ceiling was sound. It fell on a wedding and eleven people died, and the court took his ears. He cast himself a new pair out of stucco, sealed the seams with isinglass, combed his hair forward, and took the packet to Ischia, where the same law is cut into a tower in letters a foot high: Who swears falsely shall lose his ears.

“Corrado Vessa’s ears were made of stucco, and he was proud of them.” The first line of chapter 1. Read chapters 1 to 3.

The book

  • One lie, then a herd. The title is the island’s inscription (“No ear is cut twice”), and the book follows what happens when a man who cannot be caught lying lets one false thing grow.
  • Part One, The Lie (34,187 words, chapters 1 to 13) runs from the packet to Martinmas and Nine Years later, through The Lemon Court, The Counting House, The Left-Handed Shears, Three Days and The Terms.
  • Part Two, The Herd (19,492 words, chapters 14 to 21) spreads the lie through A Hand Nobody Has Seen, Lime, Arithmetic, A Letter from the Mainland, The Bad Winter, What the Cat Knows.
  • Part Three, The Collection (41,149 words) opens on an interlude, Pastore, and runs from The Annunciation through The Audit, A Very Large Oath and The Reckoning to The Quay.

The people and the place

  • Corrado Vessa, thirty-four, once the best ceiling man in the Chiaia. He knows plaster the way a washerwoman knows water, and he treats the seams as the only honest thing about him.
  • Pia Scotto, eleven or twelve, sells eggs from Fontana and asks if he is a gentleman. By chapter 9 she has walked up to his court with a cat in her arms and decided to be useful.
  • The old man with the shears who mends nets on the quay, and the raven on the last bollard and the gray cat with a white bib, who all look at his temples before anyone else does.
  • Ischia itself: dry-stone terraces, a town of white cubes under Epomeo, springs with a faint mephitic breath, and a tower at Forio with the law cut into it.

What I think is worth looking at

  • The trade does the talking. Plaster, lime, lath and dry-stone walls carry the themes: a ceiling that looks sound, a wall held up by the hill it holds back, an ear that needs one drop of blood because plaster alone looks like plaster.
  • The structure is deliberate. Three parts of very different lengths (34,187, 19,492 and 41,149 words) give the story a long setup, a short compression and a long reckoning.
  • It is built to travel. The contents lists an Adaptation Map at the back, alongside the launch kit.

How it was made

  • Plan, then draft. The plan and outline came first. Claude then drafted the book part by part from them, with me directing and editing.
  • A style pass then produced a typeset edition with a title page, the island inscription as epigraph, a contents page and page numbers.
  • The AI use is stated on the title page: “Drafted with the assistance of an AI model (Claude, by Anthropic) under the author’s direction.”
  • A launch kit of 3,159 words: a master prompt, 14 image prompts and a bank of copy. Chapters 1 to 3 are free to read above.

The game

  • Eight tracks around a cat’s world: a garden grand prix, a sunset beach, a toy track across a kitchen floor, a moonlit fish market, a living room, snowy peaks, a pumpkin patch and a canyon of cardboard boxes.
  • Drift to go faster: hold drift through a corner and the sparks go blue, orange, then purple. Let go for a mini-turbo.
  • Three cat-sized items: a fish for a boost, a hairball to throw, and a pickle to drop behind you as a trap.
  • Six racers, three laps, with computer drivers that take the racing line, drift and use their items, and a second player in split screen.

How it is made

  • Every model is a script. Cats, karts, items and scenery are built in Blender by code, with parts named so the game can colour each cat and spin the wheels.
  • A plasticine shader gives everything lumps that re-roll a few times a second and tabby stripes, so it looks hand-made.
  • Tracks are generated from a loop of points: road, curbs or toy rails, hills, ramps, boost pads and scenery, with repeated props drawn in batches so it stays smooth on a phone.
  • Touch controls laid out from the phone's own screen size, plus sounds, a pause menu and a how-to-play screen.
How it was built

Built with Claude Code in Godot 4 and Blender. I picked the cats, the tracks and the feel, and played each build; Claude wrote the game, generated the models and tracks, mixed the sound effects from credited Freesound clips, and exported the Mac app and the web version.

Brinewake

browser tactics game

An original turn-based tactics game for the browser, in the tradition of collectible-card tactics games: you build a warband from cards, deploy units onto an isometric battlefield with height, and fight over lighthouses on a drained seabed where the lifted sea is held back by their light. Eight factions, each with its own eight-mission campaign, plus skirmish and a card collection.

The game

  • Build a warband from cards: every faction has 12 champions, 3 heroes, 8 spells and 6 pieces of equipment, paid for with Tide, a resource that builds up each round.
  • Tactics on a board with height: units spend action points to move and use abilities, stairs and walls shape every fight, and the camera rotates in four directions.
  • Rules that keep a fight moving: you draw two cards a turn, a fallen champion spills Tide on its tile for whoever reaches it first, and a spent card rests for a few turns (longer the more it cost) before it comes back to your hand.
  • Eight factions with their own voice, from the Salt Concord (“Order is the only thing between the Rim and the salt”) to the Hadal Court (“The sea was ours. We are coming up to collect it”) and the Gyre (“Wherever there is a battle, there is carrion. The carrion is ours”).
  • Eight campaigns, one for each faction, each eight missions long with its own commander and story, from the Salt Concord’s The Light at Lantern Shoal to the Gyre’s The Carrion Wheel and the Hadal Court’s The Weight Returns. Missions have reinforcements, turn limits and objectives, and unlocked units carry forward. Plus skirmish, standalone scenarios and a collection.

How it is made

  • A rules engine with no screen code in it. Every unit, ability, status, terrain type, map, mission and campaign is a data file, so new content drops in without touching the engine.
  • A computer opponent that plans each turn, and a test suite that plays AI-against-AI battles for every faction to catch broken or unbalanced content.
  • Its own world and look: a world bible and art direction (a drained harbor, muted salt and bone colors, one warm light per scene) that every unit, card and battlefield follows.
  • Its own sprite pipeline: every unit is sculpted in code as a small 3D model, lit, cel-shaded and painted down to a pixel sprite sheet, so one model drives every animation.
  • A hand-built isometric renderer in the browser canvas, with animation and a full menu flow.
  • Sound from real recordings: ambience, music, unit voices and interface sounds built from credited CC0 and CC BY packs (Kenney, Meta Open Sound), with nothing synthesised.
How it was built

Built with Claude Code in TypeScript. I set the world, factions and art direction and played each build; Claude wrote the engine, the AI opponent, the renderer, the sprite pipeline, the sound mix, the content and the tests. The painted card art was made in ChatGPT from prompts Claude wrote for every card, matched to each unit's in-game model.

Glyphdeep

text-mode dungeon crawler

How the picture is made

Each frame renders the 3D world small, renders it again with flesh, eyes and loot as flat colours, boils both down to one pixel per character cell, then a shader draws the matching glyph full screen. Floors are carved into rooms and corridors on a grid and built into chunked meshes, so the game stays fast enough for a web build.

The game

Floors get bigger as you go down, with more of five monster types: Crawlers, Mimics posing as chests, Blooms on the ceiling, Widows and the Choir. Relics change how you play, from a bone knife that cuts deeper to an ear that shrugs off the Choir's song. A second version was built in Unreal Engine.

How it was built

Built with Claude Code in Godot, with a demo mode that plays itself and saves screenshots and a gallery mode that lines up the monsters for portraits. I set the concept and the look and reviewed each monster.

Ozymandias in clay

poem film

How it works

C-10 10 / 11Game

SMUDGEPOT

frost-night orchard game

An orchard on a frost night, drawn in text characters. Light, refuel and drag oil smudge pots to keep the blossom above freezing until dawn, while the wind shifts and carries the heat away.

Play SMUDGEPOTRuns in the browser. Keyboard or gamepad, with sound.

The game

Three nights, each colder and windier than the last. Fuel is short, so pots can't all burn all night. The wind shifts during the night, announced 20 minutes ahead, and a pot left where it stands warms empty ground. An orchard cat wanders toward the warmest spot, so it works as a thermometer. Trees that stay too cold too long are lost.

How it works

All the game rules are written in Lua: the heat that spreads and drifts with the wind, the trees, the pots and the cat. A small layer of browser code draws the characters and plays the sounds. A scripted player that follows the wind wins, and one that never moves a pot loses, which keeps the game about reading the wind.

How it was built

Built with Claude Code: Fable planned it, Sonnet wrote it and Opus reviewed it. The Lua runs in the browser through a WebAssembly build of Lua 5.4. Sounds are real recordings from the BBC Sound Effects archive, used under its non-commercial licence.

C-11 11 / 11Game · Pets

Pet → playable character

your cat as a 3D game character

Film your cat for about 30 seconds and get back a rigged, animated 3D cat with its coat pattern, face markings and eye colour. Then play it in Knock It Off, where it jumps onto furniture and knocks things off before the timer runs out.

Play Knock It OffRuns in the browser. Pick the grey-and-white cat or the black cat on the title card. Keyboard, or touch on a phone.

What it does

You film your cat for about 30 seconds and run one command. You get back a rigged, animated 3D cat with your cat’s coat pattern, face markings and eye colour. Then you play it in Knock It Off, where your cat jumps onto furniture and knocks things off before the timer runs out. Two cats are in the game so far, made from two public test clips.

How it works

It runs on the Mac, using Apple’s on-device vision to find the cat’s outline and body keypoints in each frame. A rigged template cat is reshaped to a house-cat build and fitted to the video, then painted with the video frames. That takes about three minutes per cat. I tried AI image-to-3D side by side: it gave a good black cat but tore at the legs, lost them when grass hid them, and needs paid GPU time for every pet. So template fitting won.

Limits and credits

Mac only so far, with no phone capture app yet, and cats only. Test footage: “Cat hunting moths” by Peter Stevens (CC BY 2.0) and “Black Cat walking” by Dzkouslavia (CC0). The template rig is “Sphynx Cat – Rigged & Animated” by Animal Animator (CC BY 4.0). Full credits are in the game.
