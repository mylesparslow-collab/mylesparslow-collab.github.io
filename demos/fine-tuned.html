CORPUS — Feature Pitch

The pitch in one breath

Logline. A blocked horror novelist trains an AI on a scraped occult archive and opens the Liber Sine Fine, a book that possesses anyone who takes it in and forces them to rewrite it on human skin in human blood. Every finished page thins the wall between our world and whatever wrote the book, and what leaks through rewrites bodies, rooms and minds until seeing it is enough to break you.

The title. A corpus is the body of text a model is trained on. It's also a body. The book turns every body near it into more text.

What it's really about. What you take that isn't yours comes back for its price.

FormatSingle feature, about 100 pages
SubgenrePossession and cosmic body horror, with a techno-horror way in
ToneGrounded and contemporary at first. Then it gets wet, wrong and relentless.
RatingHard R
Budget tierLow to mid. One snowed-in farmhouse and a heavy practical-effects budget.
CompsVideodrome (flesh and machine), The Thing (bodies that won't stay bodies), In the Mouth of Madness (a book that rewrites reality), Evil Dead (2013) (the book and the gore), Hereditary (the family turned against itself)

The hook

There's no monster to point at. There's a book, and the closer it gets to being finished, the more the world around it gives way.

The Liber Sine Fine, "the book without end," is bound in human skin and written in blood. It has no author anyone can name. Anyone who takes in its words is possessed and driven to make a new copy out of the only materials it accepts. Every finished page is a little more of a door, and something on the other side is pressing on it.

What comes through isn't a creature. It's a set of edits to reality. Skin goes thin and pale like parchment, and words surface on it from underneath. Bodies fuse with the things they touch. Rooms get deeper than the house they're in. Through the gaps you catch pieces of something enormous made of bound and written flesh, slowly turning its pages. Nobody sees all of it and stays sane.

The AI is how it gets in. When Cal trains his model on the scanned book, the creepy outputs are the first warnings: a scream buried in the audio, acrostic messages hidden in the text, pictures that move between viewings. Then the machine itself starts to change, and so do the people around it.

Cal's arc. Cal starts as a man who takes. He took his brother's book, and he takes the book's pages because they're good. The book speaks to him in Evan's voice and offers him the one thing he wants: forgiveness, and a finished book, if he'll write the last page in his own skin. In the climax, Cal refuses the fake forgiveness and gives up what he took. He destroys the book, burns his career with it, and puts Evan's name back on The Hollow Season. He walks out of the fire scarred, and honest for the first time.

The rules of the haunting

The threat has no name and no face, but it follows five rules the audience can track.

  1. Take in the book and it's in you. Reading it, hearing it or decoding its hidden messages opens the door. First come nightmares and lost time. Then the possession: the eyes fill with ink, the body moves wrong, and the voice belongs to someone dead.

  2. The possessed must write the book. Every page has to be human skin, written in human blood. They use themselves first, then whoever is closest.

  3. Every page lets more through. The page count is the ticking clock, and the visions escalate in three stages:

    • Pages 1–10: wrong surfaces. Words crawl under skin. Ink beads from pores. Reflections lag a beat behind. Letters on a screen move like insects.

    • Pages 10–20: wrong bodies. Skin thins to parchment. Ribs part like a book's covers. Fingernails harden into quill nibs. Flesh grows into whatever it touches: wood, cable, glass.

    • Pages 20–40: wrong space. The garage is deeper than the house. A hallway goes down forever, lined with skin-bound books, some of them breathing. In the gaps, something vast made of bound bodies turns its pages.

  4. Seeing too much breaks you. One glimpse of the vast thing and people laugh, claw out their own eyes or start writing. The camera never shows it whole.

  5. Only fire ends it. Every page, every copy and every drive has to burn. Miss one and the door stays open.

Its signatures. The scratch of a quill inside the walls. Ink welling up where it shouldn't. A wet sound like a page turning, much too large.

What stays hidden. What wrote the book, what's on the other side and what happens at page 40. Adeline knows a little more than she says, and even she doesn't have a name for it. "It doesn't have a name. It has a text."

Beat outline

The film runs about 100 pages: a slow-burn haunting in Act One, a bloody snowed-in siege where the house and the bodies in it stop making sense in Act Two, and a fire in Act Three. It's set in a rented farmhouse in upstate New York in January, with Cal's GPU rig in the garage, a gas generator for storms and a frozen lake out back.

Act One: the haunting (pp. 1–25)

  1. Cold open: the scriptorium (pp. 1–6). SUPER: Syunik, Armenia. Winter, 1349. A mountain monastery, snowed in. The plague has emptied it. Brother Hovhannes, an old scribe with ink-black fingertips, is the last one alive. Across the room, the brothers who died are laid out under sheets because the ground is too frozen to bury them.

    By one candle he copies from a book bound in pale, freckled leather, as he was charged to do. In the margin of every page, the painted letters seem to shift when he isn't looking straight at them. Then the words on the page start to crawl, and the same words rise up under the skin of his own forearm, moving from inside.

    He takes the penknife he uses to scrape parchment and cuts them out. We see all of it. He peels a long strip of skin from his arm, stretches it on the drying frame the way he has stretched calfskin for forty years, and writes on it with a reed pen dipped in the wound. He prays the whole time, and his hands don't stop. Then his thigh. Then his chest.

    At dawn a young novice climbs up from the valley with bread. The scriptorium floor is red. Nine pages of skin hang drying on the frame. Hovhannes is on the floor, still alive, writing the tenth page on the stones in his own blood. He looks up, almost apologetic, and says in Armenian: "There was nobody else." The novice looks past him. The scriptorium is longer than it was yesterday, and far back in the dark something enormous turns a page. The novice wraps the book in his cloak without looking at it and runs down the mountain. Title: CORPUS.

  2. The cracked ordinary world (pp. 6–12). Present day. At the Ashgrove Library, a small private research library in New England, Dev the intake conservator logs the same book: the Liber Sine Fine, 40 leaves, Armenian, provenance "calfskin." Under his lamp he finds a pore, then a freckle, then a small tattoo inside the back cover. He raises his eyebrows all the way up, types calfskin and goes home. Adeline Ash, the night archivist, starts scanning it. We cut away as her monitor's text begins to crawl.

    Meanwhile Cal is eight weeks from a deadline he can't hit. He has Tess, his rig GHOST in the garage, a box of Evan's journals he never opens, and a narration voice he quietly trained on Evan's voicemails. Tess warns him the old garage wiring can't take the rig's heat. Marcus, on a video call, teases him for building a computer instead of writing.

  3. Inciting incident (pp. 12–15). Cal buys a bundle of scanned archives from Jonah, including the Ashgrove scans, which went online the morning Adeline was taken away in an ambulance. One codex won't turn into readable text, and Cal trains on it anyway. At 3 a.m. the fans scream and the lights die. On the dark monitor, the training graph keeps drawing by itself, and it's drawing a page. In the morning GHOST writes the best pages of Cal's life.

  4. The post (pp. 15–18). Cal posts a story online under his own name. It blows up. Tess reads one line off the stats: Indexed by 14 crawlers. "Even the robots like it." That's the only nod until the last scene.

  5. Small signs (pp. 18–25). The audiobook test has a scream buried under Evan's narration. The first letters of a chapter's sentences spell NOT YOURS. In every generated cover image, a figure at a desk is a little further along, and its desk looks more and more like their kitchen table. Biscuit, the dog, won't go into the garage. At night, a quill scratches through the walls.

  6. Point of no return (p. 25). Tess views the scream as a spectrogram and finds a picture of their bedroom: two people in bed, and the one on Cal's side is open down the middle like a book. She turns around in the real bedroom. Cal is standing at the foot of their bed, asleep, holding a box cutter.

Act Two: the siege (pp. 25–75)

  1. First page (pp. 25–32). Cal wakes in the garage mid-cut. A square of skin is half-lifted from his thigh, and he finishes peeling it before he understands what he's doing. Page one is pinned to the workbench, written in his blood. That night Biscuit goes missing. In the morning Tess finds him in the snow, and his fur has grown into a neat binding, stitched along his spine. He's still breathing.

  2. The ward (pp. 32–40). Cal visits Adeline on a secure psychiatric ward, strapped down, her bandages seeping ink as well as blood. In lucid flashes she gives him the rules: every page lets more through, don't look at the big thing, and only fire ends it. "It doesn't have a name. It has a text." When Cal leaves, she's laughing, and the words on her bandages are rearranging.

  3. Jonah (pp. 40–45). Jonah video-calls in a panic. Everyone he sold the scans to has stopped answering. Mid-sentence his face starts to come apart along neat ruled lines, like pages separating, and every layer underneath is covered in writing. He's still talking. The feed freezes.

  4. Marcus (pp. 45–52). Marcus drives up to wipe the rig and calls it a stress breakdown. Alone in the garage at night, he puts his hand on the rig to pull a drive, and it won't let go. In the morning Cal finds him grown into the machine: cables under his skin, his ribcage opened around the GPUs like a book's covers, the fans turning inside his chest. His mouth is still moving. GHOST's monitor prints whatever he's trying to say. The book can change flesh now.

  5. Midpoint (pp. 50–55). In Evan's voice, through the house speakers, the book tells Cal what he already knows. Cal opens Evan's journals and we learn that The Hollow Season is Evan's book. It chose Cal because he's a thief, and thieves bargain.

  6. Snowed in (pp. 55–65). A blizzard closes the roads and takes the power. They run the generator. Diane arrives just before the road shuts, furious and wanting her pages. Tess has been humming the scream all day. The house starts going wrong. The upstairs hallway has one more door than it should, and behind it is a staircase going down past where the basement ends.

  7. Diane (pp. 65–70). Tess is fully possessed: ink-filled eyes, a body bending wrong, Evan's voice coming out of her mouth. Diane runs, takes the wrong door and ends up on the endless staircase. Cal follows her screams down past shelves of skin-bound books, some of them breathing. He finds her at the bottom, unfolded: her body opened and spread like a manuscript on a lectern, every surface written on, her eyes still tracking him. Behind her the dark goes on forever, and something in it is turning a page the size of a building. Cal looks for one second too long, and loses an hour.

  8. All is lost (pp. 70–75). Cal wakes strapped to his own workbench. The count is 39 pages. Tess stands over him with a scalpel, ruling margins down his chest. The garage walls have gone soft and fleshy and lined with text, like the inside of a mouth. In Evan's voice, everything around him speaks: "Write the last page yourself and you can keep the book. You can keep me. I forgive you."

Act Three: the fire (pp. 75–100)

  1. The break (pp. 75–80). Cal says yes, to buy a second. When Tess cuts his strap so he can write, he grabs the scalpel and fights her off. He doesn't hurt her, and she gets hurt anyway. He drags her into the house and barricades the door while the house rearranges itself around them.

  2. The climax (pp. 80–92). Cal's plan uses everything planted: the overheating rig, the bad wiring and the generator's gas. Possessed Tess hunts him through a house that keeps changing shape. Cal hauls every page, the rig and what's left of Marcus into the garage, douses it all and lights it. The garage tears open, and through the hole the vast thing presses toward him: a wall of bound flesh, and somewhere in it, Evan's face written in a hundred hands, asking him to stay. Cal answers in his own voice: "It was never mine. I'm giving it back." He doesn't look at it. He keeps his eyes on Tess. The fire takes the pages, and the door closes like a book slammed shut. The ink drains out of Tess's eyes. Cal drags her out through the snow as the garage and half the house go up.

  3. Spring (pp. 92–96). Cal has burn scars and Tess has healing margins down her back. He publishes a confession and puts Evan's name on The Hollow Season. Sometimes the words on a cereal box shift when Cal isn't looking straight at them. Tess hasn't decided whether to stay.

  4. The sting (pp. 96–100). A teenage girl in another town asks a chatbot for a scary story. It reads her Cal's old post in a warm, young man's voice. The first letters of its sentences spell WE ARE MANY. Under the narration, faintly, a scream. The phone's screen bulges outward, soft and warm, like skin with something pressing on it from the other side. She doesn't drop it. She leans closer. Cut to black.

The ending and why it works

Cal burns the book out of his house by giving back what he stole and refusing to look at what's on the other side. A final sting shows it has already found another door.

  • It's earned. The fire uses things planted in Act One: Tess's warning about the wiring, the overheating rig and the generator. Adeline gives him the two rules that win the fight: only fire ends it, and don't look.

  • It costs something. Marcus, Diane and Jonah are gone in ways nobody will ever explain. Cal has burns and no career, and words still shift when he isn't looking. Tess might leave.

  • The monster and the wound fall together. The book tempts Cal with his brother's face and forgiveness, and Cal beats it by refusing to take one more thing that isn't his.

  • The sting is planted. The "14 crawlers" line on page 17 is why a chatbot in another town knows Cal's story. The bulging screen pays off Videodrome without explaining anything.

Production notes and open questions

This is a practical-effects movie, which pushes it to a low-to-mid budget, but it stays contained in one main location.

  • Locations. A snowed-in farmhouse with a garage covers about 80% of the film. We also need a medieval Armenian mountain scriptorium for the cold open (one stone interior set and a snowy exterior), the Ashgrove library, a psychiatric ward and a staircase-and-stacks set for the impossible space, which can be one built set shot from different angles.

  • Cast. Eight speaking roles: Cal, Tess, Marcus, Diane, Jonah, Adeline, Dev and Brother Hovhannes, whose one line is in Armenian. A novice in the cold open has no lines. Evan appears only in video and voice.

  • Practical effects. Animatronic and prosthetic work for Marcus fused to the rig, Diane on the lectern, the bound dog and Jonah's face. Skin-peel prosthetics for Hovhannes and Cal. Fleshy wall panels for the garage. Contortion work for the possession.

  • VFX. Text crawling under skin and on screens, the vast thing seen only in fragments and silhouette, and the bulging phone screen.

  • Rating. This is a hard R. The self-flaying is on screen, and the body horror is shown in full.

Questions for you. I picked a default for each so the pitch holds together. Change any of them.

  • Tess. She survives and is freed by the fire. She could die in the climax instead, which is darker and more traditional.

  • The vast thing. It's only ever seen in fragments. Do you want one full reveal, maybe in the fire?

  • Biscuit. Is a body-horror dog that's still breathing too much, or just right?
