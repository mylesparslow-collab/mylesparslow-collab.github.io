Where the largest plactic-centralizer coefficient sits

The conjecture places the peak at ⌈n/2⌉. It first fails at n = 10, and the peak index divided by n tends to 1/(2 ln 2) ≈ 0.7213.

How the proof goes

A word commutes with 1 exactly when its longest weakly increasing subsequence is as long as its count of ones (Sagan and Zhao). Packing the alphabet turns the coefficient row into a weighted count of packed words. Unrestricted packed words have exponential generating function 1/(1 − t(ez − 1)), and an explicit injection shows the commuting words grow at the same exponential rate. That gives a limiting log-moment function for the peak index. Its derivative at zero is 1/(2 ln 2), and a Markov-inequality argument traps every maximizing index near that fraction of n.

The n = 10 row, 0, 1, 250, 4,917, 29,648, 75,095, 88,704, 43,414, 4,861, 1, was recomputed here by row insertion and separately by the hook-length formula. Both gave the same row.

Earlier work. Sagan and Zhao (2026) proved parts (a) and (b) of the same conjecture. Defant (2026) settled two of its sibling conjectures. Neither paper addresses where the maximum sits, and they are the only two works that cite the original. OEIS has no entry for these rows.

Total domination, edge neighborhoods and the center

For every k ≥ 5 the clique-and-hub graph on 2k + 1 vertices has γt · M = 3(k + 2), which falls short of 2|C| = 2(2k + 1) by k − 4.

How the proof goes

Every vertex has eccentricity 2, so all 2k + 1 vertices are central. Each of the three edge types has exactly k + 2 vertices in its neighborhood. The hub, one middle vertex and its clique vertex form a total dominating set. Two adjacent vertices cannot do it, because their joint neighborhood has only k + 2 < 2k + 1 vertices. So γt = 3.

Earlier work. The catalogue, updated November 5, 2026, still lists #255 as open. Two neighbouring entries were refuted in 2009. #254 swaps the center for its neighbourhood. #257 fell to a family with 2m + 3 vertices and γt = 3, built much like this one. The 2007 Graffiti.pc total-domination paper does not treat #255. Desormeaux and Henning (2016) could not be read in full, but the catalogue cites them only for #243, #251 and #253.

Proved Gregory L. Simay
OEIS A208354, 2018
and Huang 2023, Remark 9 Checked: all 65,536 compositions of sizes 0 to 16 No prior work found

At most one even part, or ones in a single run

For every n, compositions with at most one even part and compositions whose 1s form one block are equinumerous, by an explicit bijection that also keeps a size statistic. Composed with one more short map, it gives the bijective proof Jia Huang asked for in 2023.

How the proof goes

Both classes are lists of ordinary blocks (one X and some Ys) with at most one special block (only Ys). On the first side X has size 1 and Y size 2. On the second the sizes swap. The counting series (1 − x)(1 − y)/(1 − x − y)2 is symmetric in x and y, so the counts agree. The bijection writes each list as a tiling of 1s and 2s, using a single 3-tile to mark where the special block sits, and reads the tiling back with the sizes swapped.

Huang's Remark 9 asks for a bijection between the even-part class and compositions with exactly one 1. The note composes the tiling map with a short second map: keep a lone 1, append a 1 if there is none, or replace a run of r ones by the pair (1, r).

Earlier work. OEIS A208354 still marks Simay's statement as a conjecture, and Grossman's 2024 tiling comment there gives no proof. Huang's Remark 9 is unanswered: the paper's one citing article is on another topic, and Huang's 2024 follow-up does not return to it.

How the proof goes

The paper's Robinson–Schensted sum for the 321 class reduces, through Newton's finite differences and Vandermonde's identity, to a rational multiple of the central binomial coefficient. Where two golden-ratio sequences meet

List the numbers ⌊nφ⌋ and the numbers ⌊n√5⌋, and keep the ones that appear in both: 4, 6, 8, 11, 17, 22, … Kimberling conjectured that the gaps between them are always 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6, each infinitely often. That is true, and each gap has an exact long-run share.

How the proof goes

With t = (5 + √5)/10, both membership tests can be read off a single point on a circle of circumference 10: an integer m lies in both sequences exactly when 10·{(m+1)t} falls in a union of four arcs. Moving to the next integer rotates the point by 5 + √5. A gap is the number of rotations needed to land back in the arcs, and cutting the arcs by their own rotations splits them into five pieces, one per gap from 2 to 6. Equidistribution of the rotation turns the piece lengths into the shares above.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists the gap statement as a conjecture. A 2022 MathOverflow answer by Kalmynin, on a question of Kimberling, finds how dense such intersections are but says nothing about their gaps. Older general results on gaps in Beatty intersections (Fraenkel and Holzman, 1995) predate the conjecture, but no source found derives this gap set or these shares. Google DeepMind’s formal-conjectures repository and the Omega Institute’s project have nothing on it.

Proved Clark Kimberling
OEIS A392107, 2026 Checked: first positions of all {1,2}-words up to length 11 in the residues of the primes below 3,000,000. No prior proof found

Words hidden in the primes mod 3

Write each prime’s remainder mod 3 in order: 2, 0, 2, 1, 2, 1, … Kimberling recorded where each word in the letters 1 and 2 first appears, and conjectured that every position is used infinitely often. Read literally that fails at the very start, because the prime 3 puts a 0 in position 2. The real content holds: every position from 3 on is the first occurrence of infinitely many words.

How the proof goes

Shiu proved that the primes contain arbitrarily long runs in each residue class mod 3. So the residue sequence is never eventually periodic: a periodic tail would contain a run as long as its period and would then be constant. Hence the tail starting at any position i ≥ 3 differs, somewhere, from the tail at every earlier position. A long enough word read from position i therefore cannot appear earlier, and neither can any of its extensions. Position 1 is the first occurrence of exactly one word, “2”, and position 2 of none.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists the conjecture, posted in January 2026. The Omega Institute’s AI-proof project reviewed it and declined to file the literal failure at positions 1 and 2 as a technicality; nobody has proved the intended statement. The key ingredient, Shiu’s theorem on runs of congruent primes (2000), is long established.

Proved Václav Kotěšovec
OEIS A047909, 2016 Checked: exact counts by dynamic programming, three and four letters, up to 40 copies each. Partly known

Words that spell out 1, 2, …, k

Take a word with m copies of each letter 1 to k. Kotěšovec conjectured two asymptotic formulas for how many contain 1, 2, …, k in order. Both hold, and the words that fail number almost exactly (k − 1)km: with three letters and 40 copies each, the exact count is 0.999763 × 2120.

How the proof goes

Read the word left to right, waiting for a 1, then a 2 after it, and so on. A published probability model describes each wait. The new step reads the ratio of failing words to (k − 1)km as the chance that every coordinate of a random point stays under a threshold, and a binomial tail bound shows that chance tends to 1 exponentially fast. Since the failing words are an exponentially small share of all words, Kotěšovec’s column formula follows. The write-up also derives his second formula, for a fixed number of copies and a growing alphabet; exact counts computed here match it to within 0.03% at 150 letters, but that proof was not checked line by line.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists both formulas as conjectures. The probability model is El Maazouz and Pitman’s “Bernoulli clock” (Combinatorics, Probability and Computing, 2024), and the column formula follows from it in a few lines nobody had written down. The sharp count of failing words is not stated anywhere found. An unrefereed AI-assisted report (ProveIt, October 2026) treats other limits but explicitly excludes this one.

Proved Václav Kotěšovec
OEIS A261781, 2017 Checked: the reduced generating function for columns 1 to 5, against the OEIS data. No prior proof found

The shortest recurrence for marked compositions

Column k of Alois Heinz’s triangle of marked compositions satisfies a linear recurrence with constant coefficients. Kotěšovec conjectured that the shortest such recurrence has order exactly k(k+1)/2. It does.

How the proof goes

Inclusion–exclusion writes the column’s generating function as a sum of terms with denominators 2(1 − x)j − 1 for j = 1, …, k. The j roots of each factor lie on a circle around 1 whose radius, 2−1/j, is different for every j. So all 1 + 2 + … + k poles are distinct, each has a nonzero residue, and none can cancel. The recurrence order equals the number of poles.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists the recurrence order as a conjecture. No proof turned up on the web, in Google DeepMind’s formal-conjectures repository or in the Omega Institute’s project. Kotěšovec’s second conjecture on the same entry, about the constants d(k), is not addressed.

A261781

Partial result Charles Greathouse
OEIS A261312, 2015 Checked: the upper bound from the 37 proven Fibonacci-prime indices, and the density up to 106 (0.228904). Partly known

How often a Fibonacci number avoids prime Fibonacci factors

Greathouse estimated that about 22.9% of indices n have Fn divisible by no prime Fibonacci number. The density exists unconditionally and equals ¾ ∏(1 − 1/p) over odd primes p with Fp prime, which is below 0.2289063. Whether it is positive is still open.

How the proof goes

A prime Fibonacci number Fq divides Fn exactly when q divides n, and the indices q with Fq prime are 4 and certain odd primes, so they are pairwise coprime. A classical theorem says the integers avoiding all multiples of a pairwise coprime set have a density equal to the product of (1 − 1/q). The factor ¾ comes from F4 = 3. A positive lower bound would need the reciprocals of the Fibonacci-prime indices to have a finite sum, which nobody can prove.

Earlier work. The existence of the density is an application of a classical theorem on sets of multiples, so this is a correct use of known theory rather than a new idea. No post or paper applying it to this sequence was found, and whether the density is positive stays open.

Partial result Amarnath Murthy
OEIS A090257, 2003 Proof is an analytic sieve argument; not independently checked in full here. No prior result found

Growing a prime by appending squares

Start from 1 and keep appending the smallest square that leaves a prime: 11, 1181, 1181729, … Murthy conjectured the process never stops. That remains open, but every prefix has many square extensions that come close. For each large digit length k, at least a constant times 10k/2/k of the k-digit squares give a number with at most four prime factors.

How the proof goes

Appending a square s to a prefix P gives P·10k + s, a quadratic polynomial in the square root of s. The lower-bound linear sieve shows a positive share of its values over the k-digit range have no small prime factors, which caps them at four prime factors in all. What is missing is a guarantee of even one prime value, the step the conjecture actually needs.

Earlier work. OEIS lists only the conjecture that the sequence is infinite. No sieve or almost-prime result for it turned up. The nearest classical relative is Iwaniec’s 1978 theorem that n² + 1 is often a product of at most two primes.

Proved Kurkov · Bala
OEIS A140717, A167638 and MathOverflow, 2024 Checked: all 82,500 Dyck paths up to semilength 11; 101 coefficients of Bala's series Partly known

Dyck paths: a vector algorithm and a restricted-path formula

Two 2024 conjectures about mountain-range paths. Kurkov's vector algorithm computes the peak-height polynomials of OEIS A140717, as an unanswered MathOverflow question asked. Bala's exponential formula counts paths whose runs all have length at least 2 and whose peaks all sit at odd height.

The compression behind Bala's formula. Each valley of an ordinary path picks odd or even height in the restricted path. Here one valley gives two paths, of semilength 5 and 6.

How the proofs go

The vector update counts lattice paths through a grid, earning a weight at each turn. That shows the algorithm totals valley heights. The Lalanne–Kreweras involution then trades valley heights for the peak statistic Kurkov named, and a first-return decomposition handles the second variant from the original post.

For Bala's formula, peak heights 2p + 1 and valley heights 2s or 2s + 1 shrink a restricted path to an ordinary one, so the count is Narayana numbers weighted by parity choices. Lagrange inversion turns that into the exponential.

Earlier work. The MathOverflow question has no answers, and OEIS still marks Kurkov's algorithm as a conjecture. Bala's formula is partly known. An unreviewed proof posted on November 15, 2026 for the sister sequence A119370 gives the general identity, and A167638 follows from it by substitution. That proof does not claim A167638, and OEIS still lists it as a conjecture. The new part here is the path compression and its refined count.

Proved Kimberling · Kurkov
OEIS A344960, 2024 Checked: all 131,070 binary words of lengths 1 to 16 No prior work found

Flipping the bits of a word in the golden-ratio ordering

Kimberling lists every binary word in an order built from the golden ratio. Flip a word's bits and it jumps to a new position C(n). Kurkov's formula for that position holds for every n. Most words jump forward, to roughly n1.1708, where the exponent is (5 + 3√5)/10.

one word, n from 2 to 20,000typical exponent 1.1708exponents ½, 1 and 2

Hover the plot to read a word and its flip.

How the proof goes

A word's position is a sum of Fibonacci numbers: each 0 steps the index by one and each 1 by two. Shectman's Fibonacci tree grows by the same rule, so a word's position plus one is its label in the tree. The parity recurrence A348366 walks back up the tree and writes down the opposite bit at every step, so feeding its output to the tree lands on the flipped word. That is Kurkov's formula.

A word of length d with k ones sits near φd+k, and its flip near φ2d−k. In a typical word the share of ones is 1/φ² ≈ 0.382. That gives the exponent (2 − 0.382)/(1 + 0.382) ≈ 1.1708. Unusual words can push the ratio anywhere from ½ to 2.

Earlier work. OEIS A344960 still marks Kurkov's formula as a conjecture, with no proof linked and none mentioned in its edit history. None of the four related OEIS entries, and not Shectman's Fibonacci quilt monograph, states the rank formula or anything about how far words move. Parts 1 and 3 of the monograph could not be read.

array A371567 Proved by Claude. Checked against every Dyck path up to semilength 10. No prior proof found

Dyck paths weighted by their peak heights

Take every mountain-range path of semilength n, weight each one by the product of its peak heights, and add the weights. Kurkov conjectured the total is the first column of a recursively defined array. It is, and every other entry of the array has a meaning too.

One path of semilength 5. Its peaks sit at heights 2, 3 and 1, so it adds 2 · 3 · 1 = 6 to the total.

How the proof goes

Read the array entry A(n, k) as the weighted number of ways to finish a path. The path is standing at height k + 1 just after an up step, with n up steps still to use. The next step is either another up step (weight 1), or a down step. A down step makes the current point a peak worth k + 1, and the path then slides down to some lower height and steps up again. Those two cases are exactly the two terms of Kurkov's recurrence, and the starting row k + 1 is the final peak.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists this as a conjecture. The Omega Institute's AI-proof project opened a placeholder issue for it on October 31, 2026 that mentions a withheld proof, but no proof is public.

Proved Mikhail Kurkov
OEIS A369595, Oct 2024 Proved by Claude. Checked for the first 12 rows and columns. No prior proof found

A binomial array that counts generalized Bell numbers

Each entry of the array adds the entry two places right in the previous row to a binomial-weighted sum of earlier entries. Kurkov conjectured its first column gives the generalized Bell numbers A135920: 1, 2, 7, 37, 264, 2433, … It does, and the whole array has the closed form A(n, k) = Σ t(n, m) · mk.

How the proof goes

Feed the geometric row mk into the recurrence. The binomial theorem gives back m² · mk + (m + 1)k. So the recurrence acts on these rows like a walk that either stays at state m with weight m² or climbs to m + 1. That walk is exactly what the generating function of A135920 counts, a fact Ira Gessel proved in general on MathOverflow in 2024. The new step here is the reduction from the array to the walk.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists this as a conjecture. A sister conjecture, A369527, falls straight out of Gessel's answer, so it is not counted here. Nothing covers A369595 itself.

An array that solves a differential equation

Paul Hanna's sequence A249833 is defined by a differential equation for its exponential generating function: A′ = A(1 + A log A). Kurkov conjectured that a simple integer array reproduces it. It does, because the array's recurrence is that equation run step by step.

How the proof goes

Package each row as an exponential generating function in k. The recurrence becomes a single operator, F ↦ ((1 + yey)F)′. Applying it repeatedly is the same as moving along the flow of y′ = 1 + yey. At y = 0 that flow produces exactly the equation that defines A249833.

Earlier work. OEIS still lists this as a conjecture. The Omega Institute's project opened a placeholder issue for it on November 1, 2026, with no proof posted.

Shuffle squares and the symmetries of a polygon

The conjecture says any word in the letters 0, 1, 2, with each letter used an even number of times, splits into two halves where one half is a rotation or a reflection of the other. The eight-letter word 00011022 cannot be split that way. It is the shortest counterexample, and by length 18 about one word in six fails.

The only possible split. In the red half the 1 and 2 sit on opposite sides of the necklace; in the black half they are neighbours. Rotating or flipping a necklace never changes which beads are neighbours, so neither half can be turned into the other.

How the proof goes

Both halves need the same letters: two 0s, one 1 and one 2. In 00011022 the two 2s come last and the two 1s are adjacent, so each half takes one of each, and whichever half takes the 0 after the 1s is 0102. The other half is 0012. On a circle, 0012 has its 1 and 2 adjacent and 0102 does not, which settles it. The same argument works for 0…0 11 0 22 at every length from 8 up. The paper leaves the polygon's labelling slightly open, and the conjecture also fails under the looser reading, first at length 10 with 0001110122.

Earlier work. Both arXiv versions still state the conjecture, and the 2024 revision adds it to the introduction. Google Scholar shows five citing works, all on shuffle squares, and none mentions it. Only the abstract of the authors’ separate 2023 paper in Symmetry could be read.

Conjecture 3.1 Proved by Claude, independently. Checked by brute force over the two-element field. Proved earlier

Counting nondegenerate hypermatrices over a finite field

Koprowski and Lewis conjectured a product formula for how many 2 × (k+1) × k hypermatrices over a finite field have nonzero hyperdeterminant while vanishing on a prescribed staircase of entries. The formula is right, with a two-page proof.

How the proof goes

Build the hypermatrix one column at a time. The columns chosen so far define a vector bundle on the projective line, and each new column becomes a section of it that must vanish nowhere. Because such bundles split into line bundles, the number of valid next columns turns out to be the same for every prefix. A gcd count for polynomials over a finite field supplies that number, and multiplying across columns gives the formula.

Earlier work. The Omega Institute’s AI-proof project proved this first, posting a machine-checked Lean proof on October 4, 2026 by a different route. Claude’s proof was found independently and is much shorter, but it is a re-proof, not a first resolution.

Proved Russell Mizzi
arXiv 2603.27559, 2026
TF-cousin conjecture Proved by Claude (Fable 5.2). Every proof step replayed by machine on all 6,970 cousin pairs up to 10 vertices. No prior proof found

Graphs that share a double cover

Two different graphs can have the same canonical double cover. Mizzi conjectured that whenever they do, one of them contains two separate cycles of some odd length k and the other contains a cycle of length 2k. This is true, for connected and disconnected graphs alike.

How the proof goes

Each graph is the shared cover folded in half by a symmetry, and the two folding symmetries generate a dihedral group. Collapse the cover by that group to get an orbit graph. If the orbit graph has an odd cycle, the cycle unfolds in the cover into one long cycle that projects to a 2k-cycle in one graph and, after a rotation chosen by a 2-adic argument, to two disjoint k-cycles in the other. If the orbit graph has no odd cycle, its 2-colouring builds an explicit symmetry of the cover that turns one fold into the other, so the two graphs would be the same graph after all.

Earlier work. The paper’s current version, updated November 20, 2026, still lists this as a conjecture, checked up to 9 vertices. Its one citing paper refutes an earlier form of Mizzi’s separate second claim, which is not addressed here. The Omega Institute’s project lists only that second claim and calls this one open.

Proved Mikhail Kurkov
OEIS A380944, Feb 2025 Checked here: the note’s audit re-run, 1,952 top-column values against Stirling numbers and 3,330 column values against direct convolutions No prior derivation found

A binary recurrence that hides a coupon collector

For N = n + 1, a(2m(2n − 1)) = Nm − n·n!·∑j=Nm S(j, N), with S the Stirling numbers of the second kind. Every column of these rows is an exact waiting-time probability.

How the proof goes

The row of n ones is Pascal’s row. Appending zeros multiplies a comparison row Nm·C(n, k) by N each time, and it obeys the same update except at column zero. The deficit between the two rows has a generating function that factors column by column. At the last column it is n·n!·zN/((1 − z)∏(1 − jz)), whose coefficients are partial sums of Stirling numbers. That is Kurkov’s formula.

Rescaled, each column equals the chance that a sum of independent geometric waiting times exceeds m. At the last column this is the coupon-collector time on N coupons, plus one extra wait for the column-zero boundary. So with m = N(log k + x) the normalized column tends to 1 − exp(−e−x), the Erdős–Rényi limit.

Earlier work. The coupon-collector limit is classical (Erdős and Rényi, 1961). Searches by sequence, author and formula found no earlier derivation of the identity or of the probability form.

OEIS A080692, Aug 2024
Cloitre’s determinant Checked here: the note’s audit re-run, 490 exact determinants for clipping bases 2 to 8 No prior formula found

A determinant constant, corrected

The conjectured constant 1/√2 is wrong. Exactly, a(n)/2n−1 = log n/(2 log 2) + (x − 1 − log2 x)/2, where x = n/2⌊log2 n⌋. The leading constant is 1/(2 log 2), and the correction is bounded and repeats with each doubling of n.

How the proof goes

Take backward differences of the columns. The matrix becomes almost diagonal: each later column has a 2 on the diagonal and a −1 wherever the clipping rule bites, at j = 2i. A weighted sum of rows, with weight 2−ℓ on row 2ℓ, clears the first row, leaving a triangular block and a 2×2 determinant. The same steps give an exact formula for every clipping base b ≥ 2. For b > 2 the growth is 2nn1 − logb 2, times a profile that keeps oscillating, so the normalized ratio has no limit.

There is also a short check from the recurrence already in the entry. After dividing by 2n−1, each step adds 2−⌈log2 n⌉, so each completed dyadic block adds exactly one half.

Earlier work. The leading constant follows by summing Kotěšovec’s own recurrence. Searches found no earlier statement of the correction term or the clipped-matrix family.
