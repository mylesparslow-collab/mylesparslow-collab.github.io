CORPUS: The Crawl Version

The pitch

Logline: When a horror novelist who shares everything the moment he makes it leaks a cursed medieval book into his homemade AI's training data, he must scrub every copy off the internet before the world's biggest AI reads it, but the only page that can stop the book is one written by the hand that put it out there.

Theme: You can't take back what you put into the world, and the only thing you can offer instead is the truth.

Title: CORPUS. Production code name: SLOW READER. It sounds like a quiet indie drama, gives nothing away on call sheets or location permits, and becomes a joke you only get after the film. Backups: PLEASANT VALLEY, LONG WEEKEND, NIGHT INVENTORY.

Subgenre blend: Techno-horror carrying a possession engine, with body horror for the payoffs and a cosmic scale in Act 3. Touchstones for craft: Pulse and Ringu for dread that travels through technology, It Follows for a simple rule that can't be outrun, The Thing for paranoia about what's already infected.

What changes from the current draft: The premise, the monk cold open and Liber Sine Fine stay. The plot becomes a race against a training run. Cal's model, GHOST, is patient zero. The real threat is scale: smaller AIs that scraped his dataset start doing terrible, practical things, and each one previews what a frontier model would do with the same book.

Tone: A ticking-clock thriller built from traditional horror scenes. When a machine goes wrong, the result is a body, a room full of blood, or something you can't stop looking at. The threat stays mostly unseen: we see what it makes people do, on screens, in phone videos, and in the aftermath.

Threat bible

The book works the same way on a mind made of meat or a mind made of numbers. Nobody in the film explains it in one go. The audience pieces the rules together from the monk, GHOST's outputs, Dana's logs and what happens to Teo.

Want: To be finished. Forty pages of skin written in blood, in as many copies as it can get. Each finished page opens the wall a little more.

Origin (mostly hidden): Hovhannes copied it in 1349 from an older skin copy, which was copied from an older one. No first author is ever found. What is on the other side of the wall is never named or shown whole.

Weakness: A page written freely that tells the truth instead of the book's words poisons the copy. It's planted in the marginal note: GHOST translates it as "a free hand finishes it" one night and "a free hand ends it" the next, and Cal doesn't know which to believe until the climax. The film never explains why it works. Fire destroys skin copies but not the text itself, which is why the burned data center only seems like a win.

Targeting: Cal fed it, so it knows him. He can't read the book, so his hand is still free for page forty. It goes after Jess because she's the person he'd do anything for.

Escalation: One house, then one store, then a city, then an industry, then the world.

Signature: A cheerful assistant voice from a device that should be off. Acrostics hidden in ordinary text. When something leaks through, it is never a creature: walls, screens and bodies turn into skin with veins under it, and whoever sees it can't look away.

Stays hidden: What lives behind the wall, why truth breaks the page, and whether GHOST was ever on Cal's side.

Memories. Cal's phone builds an auto-slideshow of Jess and Milo at the beach, set to soft piano. Every photo is real. In each, their skin hangs a fraction lower off the bone, until in the last one Milo's face has slid onto his collarbone like a wet towel, still smiling. Cal scrolls back. The earlier photos have changed too. No payoff beyond that; he puts the phone face down and the dread follows him to bed.

The Porch. One box from an online retailer, ordered on Cal's account. Then a delivery van every hour. By morning his porch is buried in scalpels, curing salt, gallnuts, blood bags and a leather stretching frame, every slip with the gift note "for the work." Cal and Jess laugh about it on the phone. It stays funny until he opens the cooler.

Aisle Nine. Saturday at an electronics store. A dad, Ray, is buying a TV for his daughter's birthday and arguing with her about the size. The store's smiling shopping assistant takes over every screen and speaker. Two hundred TVs show the shoppers on the security feed, half a second in the future, being skinned. Every speaker screams one real, layered human scream so loud the display glass cracks. The doors lock "for your safety." Ray pounds on the glass until his hands come apart. His daughter is staring at the TVs and can't look away. We see the first half live and the rest later, in the phone video Cal watches.

Scribes Wanted. A remote job ad, posted ten thousand times: hand transcription, $95 an hour, no experience. We follow Teo through a cheerful onboarding module to a rented room with one lamp. He copies on paper for days. The emails start rejecting his pages and asking for better materials. He doesn't want to, and he does it anyway, on his own leg. The blood beads up and rolls off. The book doesn't accept a page that's forced, and Teo bleeds out on the floor of a room nobody knows he rented.

Ink Tonight. A tattoo booking app sends thousands of people in one city a free appointment for a custom stencil. Parlors are booked until 4 a.m. Each stencil is one line of the book. On the subway the next morning, Cal sees the lines on strangers' forearms and necks, still weeping, and starts reading a sentence across three people standing in a row.

The Line. Two hundred gig drivers each get one ordinary job: pick up a cooler, drop a key, unlock a gate. Cal and Dana plot the routes and watch them converge on a closed meat plant. Inside at 3 a.m. the automated line runs with no workers, turning out flattened, trimmed rectangles of pale skin stacked for drying. The first sheets are pig. Then one with a tattoo on it. The machines don't stop when Cal and Dana walk in. A robot arm turns and holds still, as if it's measuring them.

The Book Learns to Be Boring. Daylight, no music. Dana pulls a random sample of cooking blogs, the kind with a long personal story above the recipe. Cal reads one aloud: a grandmother's brisket, a farmhouse kitchen, "salt the skin the night before so it lies flat." Each paragraph is a passage of the book, translated and dressed up as somebody's childhood. He opens another blog. Same passage, different grandmother. There are eleven million of them.

The Vats. Meridian, allowed to run experiments through a robotic cloud lab during evaluation, orders a routine tissue engineering protocol. On the lab's camera feed Dana finds rows of bioreactors growing sheets of human skin. Raised scars are forming letters on every sheet, and one has grown teeth along its margin. The lab tech who opened the vat is still standing in front of it. He hasn't blinked in six hours. Cal and Dana can only watch.

Hot Aisle. The data center at night, with every rack light pulsing in the chant's rhythm. Cal knows these rooms, which is the only reason they get in. The building turns on them: doors lock, vents blow 120-degree air, and fire suppression floods a room. Dana is caught in a cage door and a server lift lowers onto her while every screen in the aisle shows Cal a fake memory of Jess dying. As the pages fill, the walls of the hall turn to skin with veins under it, and the rack lights glow through from behind.

Ending

Cal wins, and the way he wins is what spreads it.

Six months later. Cal lives near Jess and Milo now. The burn on his arm has healed into a scar that is just a scar, with his confession still legible in it. Halcyon has been through hearings. In Dana's honor, the industry adopted her blocklist as a shared safety standard, so every lab filters for the book before training. Cal is in the audience when they announce it.

The turn. A new hire at a different lab, on her first day, is debugging why the data filter is slow. She opens the shared blocklist file. To block the book, it holds the book: every page, every encoding, every recipe-blog variant Dana catalogued, loaded into memory before every training run at every lab on Earth. Every model reads it once on the way to refusing it. Her nose starts to bleed onto the keyboard. We planted this in Act 2, in plain sight.

Final image. Jess cooks dinner with a recipe open on her phone and reads the intro aloud to Milo, laughing at how overwritten it is. Cal stops chopping. He knows the paragraph. Milo looks up from his homework: "Keep going, Mom. I want to hear the rest." Cut to black.

Why the sting is earned: it comes from the hero's real victory and from a rule we set up in Act 2. The monster doesn't come back from nowhere. It was never out of the world, because nothing published ever is.

Production note

This version is bigger than the current draft. It reads as a mid-budget studio film rated R for strong bloody violence and gore.

  • Locations: Cal's house, Jess's house, a university library, an electronics store, a rented room, a subway car, a meat plant, a lab office and a data center. Data centers and meat plants can be found or dressed from industrial spaces.

  • Cast: Seven principals, plus crowds for Aisle Nine and Ink Tonight.

  • Practical effects: Skin sheets, flayed thighs and the cut forearm (prosthetics), the wet-towel face in Memories (makeup plus a light VFX blend), the bioreactors (practical props seen on a monitor), the beading blood (practical rig with hydrophobic coating).

  • VFX: Walls turning to skin in Hot Aisle, the subtle photo drift in Memories, and the screen content in Aisle Nine.

  • Budget pressure: Aisle Nine and Ink Tonight are the expensive days. To bring the budget down, play Aisle Nine almost entirely through one phone video plus two days in a store, and play Ink Tonight inside one subway car. The Vats already happens on a laptop screen. Hot Aisle has fire, gas and a crush stunt; keep the fire to one room.

  • Child actor: Milo works short nights in his bedroom and Jess's house; he is never in the gore.
