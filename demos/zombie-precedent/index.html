Zombie Precedent

When the Supreme Court overrules one of its decisions, the old case keeps getting cited. I traced every citation to the 280 cases the Court has overruled, across every American court in Harvard's Caselaw Access Project, and had Claude models read 16,050 of them to see what the citing court was doing with a dead case.

The tail runs for centuries

Half of the citations come within ten years of the overruling. The rest thin out slowly: 29% come more than 20 years after, and 7.6% more than 50. Of the 140 cases overruled at least 50 years before 2017, 113 were still being cited 50 or more years later.

The long end is mostly a few old cases cited for the part that survived. Strawbridge v. Curtiss (1806) was overruled in 1844 and was still being cited 175 years later, nearly always for its complete-diversity rule, which outlived the overruling.

Overruled cases fade faster than their peers

Old cases get cited less as they age whether or not anyone overrules them. So I matched 218 overruled cases to 1,944 Supreme Court cases of the same age and a similar citation rate that were never overruled, and indexed each to its own rate before the overruling year.

The two groups keep pace for the first few years. From about year 6 the overruled cases fall behind and stay behind for decades: at 11–40 years an overruled case gets roughly 40–70% of the citations its match gets. How much depends on the baseline. Overruled cases spike in the years just before they fall, while the fight over them is going on, so a baseline that ends at the overruling makes the drop look bigger. The toggle switches to an earlier baseline that skips the spike.

What the citing courts use them for

Claude Haiku labelled a stratified sample of 16,050 citations, Sonnet re-read the hard ones, and a blind Opus review checked 245. Most citations to a dead case are fine. Nearly half cite it for a point the overruling left alone. Another 29% say the case was overruled.

That second habit fades. In the first two years half the citations mention the overruling. After 40 years one in eight does, and more than two thirds use the case for something that survived.

The real zombies are rare

A zombie citation uses the overruled rule as law with no sign of knowing it is dead. That label went through three or four reads: Sonnet, a second Sonnet read with a wider cut of the opinion and a stricter test, then a blind Opus review. The blind review agreed with 58% of the surviving calls. A sample of the citations the second read threw out found a few real ones it had wrongly dropped.

Putting those together: about 390 citations (95% interval 270–515) rely on a dead rule, 0.72% of the 54,488 citations whose passage could be found. That rate shows no clear trend over time.

The interval leaves out one thing. The blind review found no zombies among 151 sampled "surviving point", "acknowledges", "other grounds" and "historical" labels, but that sample is too small to rule out a much larger number hiding there.

Who cites the dead

The Supreme Court cites its own overruled cases far more than any other court does, usually to discuss them. Below it, federal courts cite them about twice as often as state high courts. Estimated reliance comes out lowest at the Supreme Court and the federal courts of appeals, though the intervals are wide and the other four types can't be ranked.

How it was made, and what it can't tell you

The list of overruled cases is the Constitution Annotated table of Supreme Court decisions overruled by later decisions (292 overrulings, 280 distinct cases). Citations come from a scan of all 40,622 volumes of the Caselaw Access Project (6.9 million case records, CC0), using only real opinions of 400 words or more, de-duplicated across reporters. The labels came from Claude Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, checked by a blind Claude Opus 5.5 review. Logged model cost: $78.

Only Supreme Court overrulingsState courts and federal circuits overruling their own precedents are not covered, and that is probably where most zombie citations live.
No lawyer has read the labelsEvery reader was a Claude model, and they can share blind spots. A New York state-law trap fooled two of them.
Data ends around 2018Recent overrulings have short tails, and anything after CAP's coverage is missing.
A sample, not a census, for labelsAbout 29% of the located citations were labelled. Counts, timing and court rates are a full census.
"Relies" is the weakest labelBlind precision is 58%, and the zero found in the large classes bounds the hidden zombies only loosely.
Partial overrulings are a definitionA case overruled on one point and cited for another counts as a surviving point, even if that other point later eroded.
