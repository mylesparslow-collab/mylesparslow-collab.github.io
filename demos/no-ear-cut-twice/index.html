No Ear Cut Twice

a novel

Myles Parslow

He who swears falsely shall lose his ears.
No ear is cut twice.Inscription on the Torre, Forio d’Ischia

Part One: The Lie

Chapter 1The Packet

Corrado Vessa’s ears were made of stucco, and he was proud of them. He had cast them himself in a rented room in the Pendino, from a mix he would never have put on a ceiling: plaster of Paris for the body, a pinch of marble dust for the bloom, ochre for the flesh, and one drop of his own blood, because plaster alone looks like plaster. A living ear is not one color. Where the cartilage thins along the helix, the light goes through it red. He had learned that by holding a candle behind the ear of his landlady’s sleeping boy and looking for longer than was decent. It took seven casts. The seventh was good enough that he stood at the glass for some minutes without deciding whether what he felt was relief. Seven years had passed since then. No one had noticed.

It was not much to be proud of, and he knew it. He kept it anyway, the way a man on a bad road keeps a coin in his fist.

He came to Ischia in the second week of October, on the Procida packet, with one trunk and his hair combed forward. The boatman had a wen on his neck and, hung over it for the evil eye, an apotropaic plait of garlic. He liked to instruct. The island, he said, had one law worth knowing, and it was cut into the Torre at Forio in letters a foot high: Who swears falsely shall lose his ears.

“It happens seldom,” he said. “People are careful. Do you swear much, signore?”

“Not at all.”

“Good. A man needs his ears. How else would he hear his wife?” He laughed and spat over the side.

Corrado touched his hair. It was a habit seven years old and he did it lightly. The hair lay forward over his temples in the manner of a poet, or of a man who dislikes wind. Under it, on either side, a seam ran where plaster met skin: finer than a hair, sealed with isinglass that went tacky in the heat and smelled, when he sweated, faintly of the market at Porta Nolana. He knew where each seam lay as a tongue knows the gap where a tooth was. In the Pendino he had decided that the seams were the only honest thing about him. The name, the trade, the coat could all be argued with. A seam could not; it was simply where one material stopped and another began. On one side a man, on the other the thing the man had made so that he could go on walking down a street. He kept the joint clean with glue and a comb and a vigilance that never quite slept, and at certain moments, on a stair, in a doorway, in the dark before sleep, he felt it soften, as though skin and plaster were trying to run into each other, and his hand went up before he could stop it.

The island came up out of the sea, green and brown, its summit lost in cloud. Epomeo, said the boatman. A town of white cubes clung to the shore below it, and above the town the terraces went up in steps, each held by a dry-stone wall. Corrado looked at the walls professionally, for the place where they would fail, and could not find it. No mortar. Each course was battered a little into the hill, so that the weight of the earth pressed the stones tight and the stones held the earth back; the hill and the wall were doing each other a favor, and had been for centuries. Whoever built them had not expected to be thanked. He was thirty-four. For a few months in Naples he had been the best ceiling man in the Chiaia. Nothing he had made would stand a thousand years, and he had been thanked a great deal.

* * *

He tried not to think about the ceiling, and thought about it, as he always did, in the idle hours of a crossing.

A ballroom in the Palazzo Caracciolo, plastered by his own hand with three apprentices, for a steward who wore a ring on every finger and wanted it finished for a wedding on the Saturday. On the Thursday Corrado noticed a stain in the northeast corner. It was the color of weak tea, with a darker tidemark at its edge, and he knew what a ring like that meant: water lying above the lath, the lath swelling, the little keys of plaster that grip it softening one by one in the dark. A careful man would have opened the ceiling and looked at the joists. That was four days’ work.

On the Friday the steward asked him, in front of a notary, whether the ceiling was sound. He asked the way a man asks who wants to be told yes.

“Yes,” said Corrado. It was the easiest word he had ever spoken. Afterward it was the ease he could not forgive. The word came out warm, with a small pleasure in it, the pleasure of being the one man in the room who could say it. The notary wrote it down. The steward wanted it sworn, and Corrado swore it on a Gospel, and it felt like signing for a delivery of lime.

On the Saturday, during the second dance, the corner came down. Eleven people died: the bride’s mother, two cousins, a priest, and a boy of nine who had been allowed to stay up. Corrado was in a tavern on the Via Toledo with a plate of clams, and the news reached him between the second helping and the wine, and the first thing he thought, and he would have given a hand to have thought anything else, was they’ll want to know what I said.

They did it on the steps of the Castel Capuano, before noon, so that people could watch on their way to market. The judge had a cold. The executioner was quick and not unkind. He held Corrado’s head against his chest with one arm, like a man steadying a calf, and Corrado remembered the smell of him, onions and soap, and the warm thumb pressed against his occiput. Afterward the man said, It will heal clean. Keep it dry.

He kept it dry. For three weeks he lay in a lodging house with a cloth wound round his head, and the landlady brought broth and did not look at him, and he listened to the city. It had changed. Sounds came straight at him now, flat and undivided, without the small curve the rim of an ear gives them on the way in, and he could no longer tell whether a cart in the street was coming or going. He had not known that the ear was a kindness.

Then he got up and began to think about his hands. He was a plasterer. He knew plaster as a washerwoman knows water: its temper, its thirst, how long it stays willing in the bucket. His first ear was a lump with a hole in it. The second was merely ugly. By the fourth he had stopped thinking of an ear as a shape. It was a set of small surfaces, each turned to the light at its own angle, and what made it an ear was the shadow: the dark of the concha, the hard bright ridge of the rim, the soft lobe where the light gave up. He worked from a mirror, a candle, and the side of his own head, which he could not look at for long. On the sixth he pricked his thumb, because pure white was a dead man’s white. When the seventh had cured he bound the seam with isinglass and stood before the glass, and a man with ears looked back at him.

He left Naples within the week. He did not need another name. Nobody had bothered to cancel his own.

* * *

The packet came in to Forio an hour before noon. The quay smelled of fish and wet stone, and under those a faint mephitic breath from the island’s springs, a smell he would learn to call by its name and then stop noticing. Women with baskets, men with nets, a priest under an umbrella in the sun, a mule.

On the last bollard sat a raven the size of a hen, black with a blue sheen at the shoulder, watching the passengers come off as a customs clerk watches a queue. When Corrado stepped onto the stones it turned its head and considered him with one eye, long enough that he felt the look on his skin. Beside it a gray cat with a white bib stopped washing a paw to look at him too.

Everyone looks at a newcomer. These two had looked first, before the priest, before the women, and they had looked at his temples. His hand went to his hair.

“Whose bird is that?” he asked the boatman, who was handing down the trunk.

“Nobody’s. It was there before I was.” The man wiped his palms on his trousers. “They say it collects what the Torre leaves.”

“What does the Torre leave?” The boatman’s face went flat. “Nothing, signore. The law is hardly ever used.”

Farther up the quay an old man sat on a coil of rope, mending a net with a bone needle. He was brown and stooped and white-bearded, and he did not look up; at his belt hung a pair of long iron shears, worn as loosely as the tool of any trade, and Corrado understood which trade. He turned away. Both his ears felt warm, which was not possible. That was when the girl spoke. She was eleven or twelve, in a patched blue dress, with a basket of eggs on her hip and eyes too large for her face. She had been watching a crate swing off the boat on a rope; stenciled on its side was MATTERA.

“Signore. Are you a gentleman?”

“I’m a plasterer.” “Oh.” Her face fell, and then, by an effort he could see, rose again. “But you came on the boat. And your hair is nice.”

He laughed, for the first time in a month. “What’s your name?”

“Pia Scotto. From Fontana. I sell eggs.” She said it like a title. She pointed her chin at the crate. “That’s Don Ascanio’s. Everything good goes to Don Ascanio.”

“Who is Don Ascanio?” She looked at him as a believer looks at someone who has asked what the sun is. “The richest man on the island. He has a palazzo, and a monkey on a chain, and a daughter nobody sees. He has flocks.”

“Flocks.” “Hundreds.” It was a harmless thing for a child to say. Her eyes were on his face and stayed there. She wanted to be told something, and he had come off a boat in a good coat with a poet’s hair, and nobody had looked at him like that in seven years.

The pressure came up in his chest, the one he had never found a name for. It rose like a bubble in wet plaster, the kind you chase to the surface with the float. He had called it ambition, as some men call a limp a style. It was older than the ceiling. It might have been older than he was. It wanted one thing, and the thing was a face like this one, turned up to him as if he might be the answer.

“I keep them,” he said. “You what?” “Chief herdsman. Of all Don Ascanio’s flocks.” He lifted his chin. The words cost nothing at all. “From Gaeta to Salerno. Ten thousand head, and I’m told they’re very large goats.”

Pia’s basket tilted. An egg rolled to the lip and stopped. “All of them?” she whispered.7 “In a manner of speaking.” She ran. She went flat out across the quay and into the lanes, bare heels flashing, the basket knocking against her hip. He watched her pass the old man with the net, who lifted his head for the first time and followed her up the street with his eyes. Then the old man looked at Corrado, a long, plain look, and returned to his needle.

By the time Corrado had found a porter and asked for an inn, the news had gone up the hill ahead of him. He pieced it together later from several people, in several versions. Within the quarter hour the whole waterfront knew that Don Ascanio Mattera’s chief herdsman had landed on the noon packet: a gentleman with a poet’s hair, who also plastered ceilings for the king.

On the bollard the raven shifted from foot to foot. The cat looked up at the thin place at his temple where the hair parted in the wind, and sneezed.

Chapter 2The Lemon Court

The summons came before he had unpacked his trowels.

He had taken a room above a chandler’s in the Via Marina: one window, a bed that sloped toward the door, a smell of tallow coming up through the boards. He was kneeling at the trunk when someone knocked. On the landing stood a thin young man in a gray coat too good for the stairwell, with a clerk’s stoop and ink on his cuff.

“Bonifacio Ruocco,” he said. “Secretary to Don Ascanio Mattera. Don Ascanio presents his compliments and asks whether his chief herdsman will honor him at dinner.” “I’m not sure I am anyone’s chief herdsman.” The young man looked at him without any expression at all. “That is what the town says. Don Ascanio would like to hear it from you.”

Refusing would have cost nothing. Corrado came back to that many times, in the dark, with sulphur in his clothes. He could have said there has been a misunderstanding and watched the secretary’s face close like a ledger. He could have been on the evening packet. The misunderstanding would have lasted a week and been forgotten, as misunderstandings are on islands, where there is little else to eat.

“Give me a quarter of an hour,” he said. He washed. He put on his one good coat, brown, cut for a larger man in a richer decade. In the glass over the washstand a plasterer looked back at him, and he practiced, briefly, the chin of a herdsman. Then he followed the secretary up the hill.

* * *

The road climbed between the terraces, so near the walls that he could see how they had been made: each stone chosen for the face it would show and the bed it would lie on, and the small ones, the pinners, driven into the gaps afterward to lock the rest. He laid a hand on one as he passed. It was warm, and faintly gritty, like the crust of a loaf.

“The Scotto terrace,” said Ruocco, without turning. “Third on the left. Or it was.”

“Was?” “A figure of speech.” He said nothing more until the gate. The Palazzo Mattera was pink: limewash over limewash, so many coats that the arrises had gone soft and the cornices looked iced. It stood on a shelf of the hill with the whole bay below it, and its front was all shutters, none of them open. Through an arch Corrado saw a court of cobbles with a well at its center and lemon trees in tubs along three walls. They were old trees, heavy with fruit, and the light that came down through them was green and aqueous and moved a little when the leaves did. The air smelled of rind and blossom. Against the far wall something crouched in the shade, and it took him a moment to see that it was an animal, and that it was chained.

“The monkey,” said Ruocco. “Zenobia. Don’t approach her unless she approaches you.”

She was a Barbary macaque, tailless and brown, the size of a large dog, with a face like a dried fig and hands that were disturbingly a woman’s. Under one arm she held a pale, nearly hairless thing the size of a loaf, and under the other, ignored, a second. She had given birth, and recently. The two infants mewed and clung. The mother’s eyes went with him across the cobbles, dark and bright and very old.

“Twins,” said a voice. “In April. Is it not a miracle? The apothecary says she is eleven, which is old, and that the climate is wrong for her. She ought to have died. But she was lucky.”

* * *

Don Ascanio Mattera sat under the largest lemon tree in a chair with arms, a table before him and a pot of coffee on the table. He was near fifty, short, round-faced and pale, with soft hands and a beautiful voice. A voice like a well-oiled drawer, Corrado thought, and was surprised by the thought: it slid open without a sound and gave up exactly what was asked of it, and no more.

On his head sat a black velvet calotte, embroidered at the rim, a cap for the study and not the garden. In the heat of the court a bead of sweat ran from under it into his collar, and he did not take it off. He rose an inch from the chair with a soft ah and held out a hand, and Corrado found that his own hand had gone up to his hair.

He lowered it. “Signor Vessa. You will forgive my not coming to the quay. I have not walked that far since the Bourbons came back.” The smile widened by a degree. “Please. Sit. Take coffee. I am told you are a man of many trades.”

“I’m a plasterer, Don Ascanio.” “And a herdsman.” “In a manner of speaking.” “Yes.” Mattera gave him a look of such kindly, unhurried attention that Corrado’s stomach shifted. “I have heard that phrase twice today. The Scotto child brought it up the hill. She says you said it with great style.” He poured coffee into a cup of real porcelain, thin enough that the level of the coffee showed through its side. “I thought: there is a man who understands the economy of language.”

Corrado took the cup. His hands were steady. He had learned that in Naples, under the executioner’s thumb: whatever the face does, the hands can be kept still.

“Don Ascanio, I’m afraid there has been—” “A misunderstanding. Of course. They happen.” Mattera lifted one finger. “But allow me a story first, before you explain. I have a gift for stories and few people to give them to. My son has no interest in them. My daughter is indisposed. And my secretary has heard them all.” He settled back. “Eleven years ago, at the Casamicciola fair, I had a cow. A big red animal, handsome, sound in wind and limb, and barren as a stone. A farrier, a veterinarian and a priest had all agreed on it. For three fairs I stood in the dust of the cattle ground and watched the farmers walk round her, feel her flank, look at her udder, and walk away. They knew. Everyone knows a barren cow. It is something in the eye.”

He drank. “At the fourth fair I swore to a buyer, on my mother’s grave, that she was in calf. I had no idea whether it was so, and I did not care. I said it with such conviction that I very nearly believed it myself, and he paid the first price of the day. My son held the halter. He was eleven. He has never forgiven the cow.”

“And the cow?” “Barren, naturally. The man cursed my name for a year.” The smile was gentle. “A small matter. I tell the story often because it contains everything I know. A promise is worth more than a fact, Vessa. A fact can be checked. A promise must be waited for. Nothing on this earth sells like a thing not yet delivered.”

He set down the cup. “Now. You told a child that you keep my flocks.”

* * *

Corrado took stock of the court: the macaque, no longer mewing at her infants, watching him; the gate thirty paces at his back; the secretary, who was not leaning on the wall by accident.

“I did, Don Ascanio. It was foolish. I apologize. I came off the boat tired, and the child was so—”

“Hungry,” said Mattera. “Yes. They are, on this island. Do you know how many goats I own?”

“No.” “Seventeen.” The word fell into the green air like a stone into a well. “They graze in the field behind the chapel. A boy minds them. They are unremarkable goats. But the town has believed for twenty years that I have flocks beyond counting, and do you know why?”

“No.” “Because I have never said so. Not once.” Mattera spread his soft hands. “I permit a thing to be assumed, and then I behave as though it were true. I give the clerk a better coat. I buy salt by the cartload. I allow it to be remarked that the pastures of Barano are my pastures, and I do not correct it. Reputation is a kind of architecture, Vessa. One does not build it out of lies. One builds it out of the absence of correction.”

Corrado said nothing. “And into this structure walks a stranger. A gentleman. With hair.” He tilted his head, and for a moment the soft face could not be read. “A man who says, before witnesses, that he is my chief herdsman, with ten thousand head. Do you know what I thought when I heard it? I thought: how extraordinarily convenient. I have needed such a man for years. I have never found one willing to say it aloud.”

The coffee in Corrado’s cup had begun, very slightly, to shiver. He set it down.

“You want me to go on saying it.” “I want to employ you.” Mattera drew a folded paper from his sleeve. “Ten years. Eight scudi a month, which is a generous emolument; a room in the east wing; board; a purse in advance. In return you will be what you said you were. Herdsman of all my flocks, present and future. You will walk the quay in a good coat. You will be seen. You need never touch a goat.”

“And if I decline?” “Then you decline.” Mattera smiled. “I have no power to compel you. You may leave on the evening packet, and I shall say there was a misunderstanding, and the town will believe me, and you will be a young man who lied to a child on a pier. They will find it charming for a week.” He let a pause go by. “But in Naples, I imagine, there are people who would be very interested in a man who calls himself Vessa and combs his hair forward. I do not say there are. I say it is a city with a long memory.”

Above them the lemon leaves moved, and the green light moved with them.

Corrado felt the old bubble come up into his throat and lodge there. He thought: he knows. Then, with a clarity that surprised him: he doesn’t. He is guessing. He is doing to me what I did to the girl, saying a thing with confidence and watching my face to see whether it is true.

It did not help to see it. It meant only that they were two of a kind. And now he saw what he had not seen before, which was how the calotte sat. Velvet over a high, smooth skull, drawn low at the sides and tucked under a fringe of gray hair, worn exactly as Corrado wore his hair: forward, level, down over the place where an ear should be. Under the left edge, in the damp of the collar, there was a puckered, waxy shine that was not skin, or not skin of the ordinary kind. He knew a cicatrix like that. He had studied one in a hundred mirrors.

He looked up. Mattera was looking at him, and the face was entirely innocent.

Say nothing, thought Corrado. Say nothing, and neither of us has to know.

“The room in the east wing,” he said. “Does it have a window?”

“Two,” said Mattera, very gently. “Facing the sea.” Corrado took the pen.

* * *

He read the paper first. It was the plasterer’s reflex: never close a wall you have not looked behind. It was a good contract in a clear hand. Herdsman of all flocks, present and future, to be held by the said Mattera, within the Kingdom or beyond it. The ten years were underlined. In the margin, in a smaller and sharper script, someone had added a line: Employee to bear all consequences of his public representations.

He read it twice. It was meant to be read. Whatever he said in public would be his to answer for, and the man across the table would be somewhere else when it fell due; and to be told so plainly, in a margin, in a hand that did not trouble to disguise itself, was a kind of compliment. It meant that he was worth a clause. He signed.

“Good,” said Mattera. “Very good. Welcome, Signor Vessa. My son will want to meet you.”

* * *

The son came out of the house as though he had been waiting behind the door.

He was twenty-two, tall and badly assembled, with his father’s pallor and none of his father’s ease. His shirt was buttoned one hole off and his cuffs were chewed. He stopped three paces from the table and bowed with a jerk of the neck, looking at nobody’s face.

“Papà.” “This is Signor Vessa. He keeps our flocks.” The young man’s glance reached Corrado and slid off him. “I’m glad,” he said. “That is, I’m sure it will be—” He stopped. His father waited, with a patient, benevolent expression that made the silence worse. “I only came to say the Pretore’s clerk is here. About the harbor.”

“I know. I’ll see him in an hour.” “Yes. Of course. Should I—” “No,” said Mattera, with great kindness. “You needn’t. Thank you, my dear.”

The boy went away with his hands clasped behind his back. In the arch he paused and looked back, not at his father, not at Corrado, but at the macaque under the far wall.

She had stopped watching the strangers. She had turned to the infant under her left arm, the pale, plump, favored one, and was washing its face with long slow strokes of her tongue. The other, the thin one, clung to the hair of her flank and made a small sound like a hinge. Nobody washed it. Ruggero looked at it a moment longer. Then he went in.

* * *

Corrado climbed the stairs of the east wing in the late afternoon. The room was as promised: a bed, a table, a washstand, two windows on the sea. The walls had been whitewashed over a surface so rough that he stopped and ran his thumb across it, as a doctor runs a thumb over a swelling.

The plaster was bad. It had been laid thin and in a hurry, over a coat that had not cured, and under the whitewash it was already crazing: hairlines running up from the skirting, branching at the corners like the rivers on a map. Four more winters and it would come away from the lath.

He stood a while with his thumb on the wall. Then he opened his trunk, took out his smallest trowel and a twist of fine stuff, and began to dub out the nearest crack, because his hands wanted something to do.

Down in the court the macaque had begun to cry, a thin, almost human sound that rose and broke and rose again. He listened to it while the light went. He was a man without a past, with a title he did not hold, mending in secret a wall that was not his, and it did not occur to him that the wall was telling him anything.

He was tired, and the crack, when he had filled it, took the whitewash beautifully, and could not be seen.

Chapter 3Letters

The letters took two days to compose and an hour to seal.

Mattera dictated them in the study, a small room off the court crowded with ledgers and with portraits of ancestors he had very probably bought. He paced in his soft shoes, and the velvet cap rose and fell with him. Corrado sat at the desk with a good pen, and for the first time since Naples he was asked to do something at which he was gifted.

He had a fine hand, his one vanity apart from the ears. He had learned it from engraved copybooks in a Neapolitan schoolroom, a copperplate that ran across the page like water over pebbles, with majuscules so generously looped that they made a tradesman’s receipt look like a patent of nobility.

“The flocks of Don Ascanio Mattera,” said Mattera, “numbering twelve thousand head.”

“You said ten to the girl. I said ten.” “Twelve is better. People distrust round numbers.” He considered the ceiling. “Will graze the terraces of your commune, according to the ancient transhumance, from the feast of Saint Martin. Is ancient too strong?”

“There is no transhumance on Ischia. The goats don’t go anywhere.”

“Then it will be invented, and in a year no one will remember it was not always so.” He made a small gesture, like a conductor bringing in the strings. “Continue. The undersigned, as chief herdsman, gives notice in the manner of his office, that the commune may make provision.”

“Provision for what?” “Whatever they like.” He smiled. “Write: on my oath.” The pen stopped. “It is a form of words,” said Mattera gently. “All herdsmen use it. It is like your servant at the foot of a letter. Do you sign yourself servant to a man you mean to serve? I have never met anyone who did.”

“On this island an oath is—” “On this island the statute is for lawyers, Vessa. I am speaking of manners. There are oaths that matter, made in court, on the Gospel, before a notary, and there are oaths that are only emphasis. Nobody loses an ear for emphasis. Tarquinio himself will tell you so.”

It was the same argument that had put the stain in the ballroom ceiling and the plaster over the stain: a small distinction, offered by a man who did not need to be right to a man who badly wanted to be allowed. It settled over him like a warm cloth.

He thought of the crack in his wall upstairs, dubbed out and whitewashed, and how it would open again with the first damp.

He wrote it. On my oath.* * * Four letters went out on the Thursday packet: to Lacco Ameno, to Casamicciola, to Barano, to Serrara Fontana. Each was addressed to the Sindaco and Council. Each bore Mattera’s seal, a goat rampant, cut for the purpose that afternoon by a stationer in Forio who asked no questions and charged as though he had. Each carried at its foot, in the elegant hand, the signature Corrado Vessa, Chief Herdsman.

The tribute began the following Tuesday.

Lacco Ameno’s came first: a donkey led by a boy, two panniers of oil, a purse of silver, and a note in the Sindaco’s own trembling hand. It was obsequious in the old style. The great flocks, it suggested, could surely have no need of our poor terraces, which are rocky, and which our families have worked since the time of our grandfathers’ grandfathers; we send this small token of respect, and trust it will suffice. Corrado read it twice. Nobody had ever written to him in fear. “They are paying us not to come,” he said. “They are paying the Pasture Fund,” said Mattera, with satisfaction. “It is a service. A convenience. Think of it as a toll.”

“A toll on what?” “On the non-occurrence of an event.” He lifted the purse and weighed it, as one weighs an egg for freshness. “People pay for a great many non-occurrences, my dear fellow. In the cities they call it insurance.”

By Friday the other three towns had paid. The week after there was a second round, because the Sindaco of Barano had heard that Casamicciola had given more. Wine came, and grain, and a pig with its feet tied. A woman walked all the way from Panza with a dozen eggs and a twist of paper, and in the paper, when Corrado opened it, was a gold ring, worn thin on one side by a hand that had done a great deal of work. She would not give her name. It was for her husband’s terrace, she said; he was sick; she hoped the herdsman would look kindly on the south slope.

“I’ll look,” said Corrado. “Thank you, Maestro,” she said, and kissed his hand. Nobody had called him that in seven years. He stood in the doorway with the ring in his palm like a votive left at a shrine, and something settled in his chest that was not quite pleasure and not quite shame, heavier and warmer than either. It might have been love, of the kind that is paid to a thing that does not exist.

* * *

The nickname came by the end of the month: il Pastore Grande, the Great Shepherd. The tavern wits held that a man who kept twelve thousand goats must be partly goat himself, and debated which part, but the name was spoken with respect. When he walked the quay in his brown coat, men stepped aside and women touched their foreheads, and small boys ran ahead to announce him.

Mattera let him keep a tenth. It was Mattera’s own idea. He was, in his way, scrupulous about money.

Corrado sent his share where it might do some good: a measure of wheat to a widow whose son had drowned, a pair of shoes to the girl with the eggs. He called it restitution. It pleased him to give and it pleased the island to receive, and for a few weeks everyone’s account seemed to stand in modest and satisfactory balance. In the evenings he sat on the terrace of the east wing with a glass of white wine from the Barano tribute and watched the sun go down into the sea, and told himself he was not unhappy.

He did not look at the mountain. It stood above the palazzo, huge and green and indifferent, with its cap of cloud. The islanders said it had a mouth, a fumarole on the northern flank where the hot springs rose, a cleft from which on certain nights a warm wind came. They called it the Breath. They told their children not to play near it. The stationer had mentioned it to Corrado lightly, between the price of the seal and the price of the wax, as one mentions a local superstition to a foreigner.

On the ninth night after the letters went out, he woke before dawn. He lay and listened. The house was silent. The macaque in the court was silent too. The window stood open on the sea, and the air that came through it was warm and heavy and smelled of struck matches.

Then, very faintly, from somewhere beneath the floor, he heard a bell.

It was a small flat clank, the sound of bronze with a cracked lip. It rang twice. It was answered by another, a little deeper and farther off, and then a third. Under the bells was another noise, which took him some time to place, because he had never heard it at that scale: a great many hooves on stone, a long way down, moving slowly, the way a crowd moves out of a church.

He lay still, sweating. He told himself it was the sea. He told himself it was the old house settling on its foundations, as old houses do in the cool of the night.

The bells went on a long time. When the sky grayed they stopped, one by one, like candles being pinched out. A rooster crowed in Forio, and Corrado found that his hand had gone up in the dark, as it always did, to the left side of his head, where the plaster ear was. It was warm. It was warmer than his skin.

He took his hand away. He did not sleep again.
